# How to validate a business idea before you spend anything

Most ideas die of the same thing: the founder spent four months building, then went looking for someone to buy it. Validation reverses that order. You find the buyer first, and the buyer tells you what to build.

There is one rule that makes all of this simple. **Money changing hands is validation. Nothing else is.** Compliments are not. A friend saying it is a great idea is not. Likes, saves, shares and a waiting list nobody paid to join are not. Every one of those things can be true while the business fails, so none of them can be used as proof.

## Step one: name one specific buyer

Not a market. A person you could contact this week.

"People who want to get fit" is not a buyer. "Personal trainers in my city who run outdoor group classes and handle their own bookings by WhatsApp" is a buyer, because you can go and find nine of them by Sunday. The narrower you make the description, the easier every following step becomes — see [niche and ideal customer profile in the glossary](/founder-glossary/niche) for why narrowing usually widens your chances.

If you cannot name the buyer, you do not have an idea yet. You have a category.

## Step two: find out what they already pay for

Before you propose anything, establish that money is already moving. People rarely start paying for a brand new category; they switch from what they use now. So look for what they currently spend on — a tool, a freelancer, a staff member's time, a workaround they hate.

The best source here is complaints, not surveys. Read reviews of whoever serves them badly. Read the replies under posts where they vent. A person describing exactly what is wrong with their current option is telling you what to sell and what to charge.

## Step three: ask for the sale, not for an opinion

This is the step everyone avoids, and it is the whole exercise.

Go to ten of those buyers and describe what you will do for them, when, and for how much. Then ask for the money. Not feedback. Not a call to discuss it. The money, or a deposit, or a signed commitment to pay on delivery.

You are looking for one of three answers:

- **Yes, here's the money.** You have validation and a first customer in the same breath. Start work.
- **No, because...** The reason is worth more than the sale. Two or three people giving the same reason is the shape of your real offer.
- **Maybe later, send me details.** Treat this as a no. It is the polite version, and building on it is how founders lose a year.

Ten conversations is enough to see a pattern. If nine say no for nine unrelated reasons, your buyer description is still too wide — go back to step one.

## Step four: deliver by hand before you automate

Your first delivery should be manual, unscalable and slightly embarrassing. Do the work yourself, in a spreadsheet, over WhatsApp, at whatever hour it takes.

This is not a compromise. Doing it by hand is how you learn which parts customers actually care about — and it is almost always a different list from the one you would have built. Automation before that point builds the wrong thing very efficiently.

## What validation does not tell you

It does not tell you the business is a good one. An idea can be validated and still be a bad business, because one customer paying does not mean the numbers work at scale.

That is a separate question, and it is arithmetic rather than conversation. Once you have a price and a cost, run them through the [break-even calculator](/calculator/break-even) to see how many sales the thing actually needs, and the [customer acquisition cost calculator](/calculator/customer-acquisition-cost) once you have spent anything at all on finding buyers. A validated idea with broken [unit economics](/founder-glossary/unit-economics) is a validated way to lose money.

## Where to start

Validation is fastest on ideas where you can deliver the first job yourself, this week, with what you already own. That is most of the [zero-investment business ideas](/category/zero-investment-business-ideas) here: a service someone needs, no stock, no tools to buy.

Two worth reading as worked examples: [Clear History Reference Verifications](/idea/landlord-reference-check-calling-rental-service), where the buyer is a landlord who already does this badly himself, and [Clear Line Business Proofreading](/idea/business-email-proofreading-non-native-speakers-service-remote), where the buyer already knows the problem and can be reached in a day. Both blueprints name who pays, how the money works, and what hurts in year one — which is the same four questions validation is trying to answer.
