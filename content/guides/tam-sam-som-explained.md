# TAM, SAM and SOM explained without the spreadsheet theatre

Three initials that get used to make small plans look big. Understood properly they do something more useful: they force you to admit how much of a market you can actually reach.

## The three numbers

**TAM — total addressable market.** Everything everyone who could ever buy what you sell spends on it in a year. It is a ceiling, and the number most often inflated on purpose.

**SAM — serviceable addressable market.** The part of that you could realistically serve, given where you are, what language you work in, and what you can physically deliver. A knife-sharpening round cannot serve a whole country, however large the country's kitchenware spend is.

**SOM — serviceable obtainable market.** The share of the serviceable part you expect to win, given who else is already there. This is the only one of the three you should plan against.

Each is the one above it, narrowed. Definitions for all three sit in the [founder glossary](/founder-glossary/total-addressable-market).

## How to work them out

Four numbers, and every one has to be something you can point at.

1. **How many buyers exist.** Count them from something real — a directory, a trade association list, a census figure, the number of shops on a street you walked down. Not a feeling.
2. **What one buyer spends a year** on this kind of thing, to you or to anyone.
3. **What share you can reach**, honestly, given your location and capacity.
4. **What share of those you think you can win**, against the people already serving them.

Buyers times annual spend is your TAM. Narrow by the reach share for SAM, then by the win share for SOM. The [market size calculator](/calculator/market-size) does the arithmetic and shows the working, so you can check every step by hand.

## Where market sizing goes wrong

**Starting from a big published figure and slicing it.** "The market is worth thousands of crores and we only need one per cent" is the most popular sentence in bad plans. One per cent of a huge market is not a smaller target, it is an unspecified one. Nobody knows which one per cent, or how you reach them.

**Counting people who cannot buy from you.** If you deliver in person, buyers outside your travel radius are not in your SAM. Removing them makes the number smaller and the plan real.

**Optimism in the last percentage.** Pick the win share as though a competitor is already doing this well, because one probably is. A small SOM you can defend is worth more than a large one you invented.

**Guessing the buyer count.** If you genuinely cannot count the buyers, market sizing cannot help you yet. Go and count. That counting is the research, not the multiplication afterwards.

## What it is actually for

Not for a pitch deck. For two decisions.

The first is whether the market is big enough to be worth your year — and for a one-person business the honest answer is usually yes, because you need very few customers. The second is more useful: sizing makes you name exactly who the buyers are and where they are, which is the same work that [validation](/startup-guides/business-idea-validation) needs anyway.

If your SOM looks too small to bother with, that is information. Either the [niche](/founder-glossary/niche) is too narrow, or the price is too low, or this is a side income rather than a business. All three are fine to know before you start rather than after.
