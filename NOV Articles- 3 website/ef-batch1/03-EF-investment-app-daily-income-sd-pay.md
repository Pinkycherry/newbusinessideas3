# An investment app that promised daily income across Gujarat, and why shopkeepers keep joining these plans first

## A boat near Harishchandra Ghat

We reached Kashi in the late afternoon, tired in the good way that only a long train journey and a familiar city can make you tired.
Shankar Kaka's four-wheeler was waiting at his house, dusty and patient, and his mother, whom everyone calls Amma, had kept rice, dal and a sabzi warm for us as if we had only gone out for an hour.
After the meal, Kaka said there was only one place to go on the first evening, and we both knew which one he meant.
By the time the sky turned the colour of copper, we were sitting in Bhola's boat a little away from Harishchandra Ghat, the oars resting, the fires of the cremation ground burning steadily on the bank.
I have sat on this river many times, and I have never once felt that the fires were sad.
They are honest.
Every person who reaches that ghat arrives with nothing in the hands, and every evening Lord Shiva's city reminds you of it without saying a word.
That is where the third story of this journey reached me, and I am writing it the next morning, on the steps, after a night of very little sleep and a great deal of thinking.
Kaka had invited an old acquaintance to join us in the boat.
His name is Hasmukhbhai, and he runs a kirana and farm-supplies shop in a small town in Amreli district in Saurashtra.
He had come to Kashi with his elderly mother for darshan, and Kaka had helped them find a place to stay in a dharmashala run by people from our side.
Hasmukhbhai is a large, soft-spoken man who folds his hands every time the boat passes a temple, and for the first half hour he spoke only about his mother's knees and the steps of the ghats.
Then Kaka asked him how the festival season had gone back home, and his face changed.
"Business was fine," he said. "But the town was not fine. Half my customers had money stuck in one investment app."

## What the Amreli police investment probe says about SD Pay app fraud

The app he named was SD Pay, which he said was also known in his area as Apna Pay.
I had read about it in passing, and while Bhola rowed us slowly upstream, I opened the regional reports.
A Gujarati report in [Sandesh's Amreli section](https://sandesh.com/gujarat/maru-saher-maru-gaam/news/amreli/amreli-crime-branch-arrests-sd-pay-owner-dhaval-patel-634-crore-fraud-odisha) says that the Amreli district police and crime branch have arrested two alleged main operators of the SD Pay app, the app owner Dhaval Patel and his associate Hiren Shah, from Sambalpur in Odisha.
According to that report, the investment app attracted lakhs of investors across Gujarat with the promise of high daily returns and referral bonuses, collected crores of rupees, and then suddenly shut down.
That report puts the figure linked to these two accused at ₹634 crore.
Earlier reports across Hindi, Gujarati and national outlets placed the total collected through the app network at about ₹1,211 crore.
As always, these are police allegations at the stage of investigation, and the accused will have their own defence in court.
What matters for a small business owner is the structure of the offer, and Hasmukhbhai knew that structure very well, because he had watched it travel across his own shop counter.
The amreli police investment case has become a household topic in his district.
"Everyone is asking the police for one thing," he said. "When will the money come? And the police are asking everyone for one thing. Where did you send it?"
Bhola, who had been listening without turning around, said quietly in Bhojpuri that the river also asks the same question of every boat: where did you come from, and where are you going.
Kaka translated, and Hasmukhbhai smiled for the first time that evening.

## A daily income investment plan, explained as maths

I asked Hasmukhbhai what return the investment app had shown his customers.
He said the numbers varied by plan, but that the promise was always a small percentage every day, credited into the app's wallet, with bigger percentages for bigger plans and extra income for bringing in others.
A daily income investment plan sounds modest when you hear it as a small daily percentage.
It stops sounding modest when you multiply it.
I took out my notebook and did the maths in the light of my phone, with Kaka holding the torch and Bhola keeping the boat steady.
Even one percent a day, without any compounding, comes to 365 percent in a year.
With compounding, it becomes a number so large that no business in the world could pay it.
A bank fixed deposit, by comparison, pays a single-digit percentage per year.
A good mutual fund in a strong year may do better, but it can also lose value in a weak one.
No legitimate product in India promises a fixed daily return.
Here is the table I drew, which Hasmukhbhai photographed to send to his town's traders' group.

| Product | What it promises | Who regulates it | Can it guarantee a fixed daily return? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bank fixed deposit | Fixed interest per year, stated in advance | RBI | No daily return; fixed annual rate only |
| Mutual fund | Market-linked returns, no guarantee | SEBI | No |
| Post office savings schemes | Fixed annual rates set by government | Government of India | No daily return |
| Shares through a registered broker | Market-linked, can rise or fall | SEBI | No |
| An app showing a daily percentage in its own wallet | Fixed daily income plus referral income | Usually nobody | It claims to, which is the warning |

Hasmukhbhai looked at the last row for a long time.
"My customers never saw it as investment," he said. "They saw it as a second shop. A small shop that earns every day while you sleep."
That is exactly how an app based investment scheme wants to be seen.
Kaka shook his head and pointed to the fires on the bank.
"Only one shop in Kashi earns every day without fail," he said, "and nobody wants to be its customer."
It was a dark joke, but it was also the truest thing anyone said all night.

## Why shopkeeper investment fraud spreads from the counter

I asked Hasmukhbhai how the app had reached his customers.
His answer was the most useful part of the evening, and I have heard versions of it in many towns.
It started, he said, with a man who owned a mobile recharge and accessories shop near the bus stand.
He installed the app, put in a small amount, and showed his daily credits to every customer who came to recharge a phone.
Within weeks, a tailor, a vegetable wholesaler, a fertiliser dealer and two school teachers had joined.
Then the investment app introduced referral income, and every one of them became a small agent.
"A shop is a meeting place," Hasmukhbhai said. "People trust the shopkeeper. If he says something is good, they believe it."
This is why shopkeeper investment fraud spreads so quickly.
The shopkeeper is not paid to promote anything.
He believes in it, and his belief carries the weight of years of honest dealing.
I have written about how [word of mouth works better than advertising for a young business](https://ethicalfounder.in/word-of-mouth-vs-ads-for-a-first-year-business/), and the sad side of that truth is that word of mouth also works better than advertising for a scheme.
Kaka added something from his own experience as a guide.
In Kashi, he said, pilgrims trust the boatman, the flower seller and the tea stall owner far more than any signboard, and that is why the city's honest people take that trust so seriously.
"If I send a pilgrim to a bad lodge," he said, "my name goes with him. A shopkeeper's name goes with every recommendation too."

## The referral income app and the customer who joins because the shopkeeper did

Hasmukhbhai himself had not invested.
He said this without any pride, almost apologetically, and when I asked him why he had stayed out, his answer was simple.
"My father taught me one thing," he said. "If money comes to the shop without goods going out, something is wrong."
His nephew, however, had joined.
The nephew runs a small mobile repair counter inside Hasmukhbhai's shop, and he had put in his savings and then some money borrowed from friends.
For a few months, the daily credits came, and small withdrawals worked.
Then, close to the festival season, the investment app withdrawal problem began: requests were "pending", then "under verification", then the app asked users to upgrade to a new version, and finally it stopped opening.
"He was going to buy a new soldering station and stock for the festival," Hasmukhbhai said. "Instead he spent the festival going to the police station with forty other people."
The nephew is young, and he will recover, but the people who joined because of him are his friends, and that is a harder loss.
A referral income app turns every investor into a recruiter, and every recruiter into a person who must face the people he brought in.
That is the hidden cost these schemes never show on the dashboard.
In my notes on [money in the first six months of a small business](https://ethicalfounder.in/money-in-the-first-six-months-of-a-small-business/), I have written that a new business owner's most valuable asset is his reputation in the first circle of customers.
A scheme spends that reputation without asking.

## The chai stall at the ghat

When Bhola brought us back to the steps, it was nearly nine at night, and Kaka insisted that no evening in Kashi is complete without tea from Munna's stall.
Munna has a small stall a short walk from the ghat, with a coal stove, a dented aluminium kettle and a radio that plays bhajans all day.
He has known Kaka for years, and when Kaka told him what we had been discussing, he laughed and took out his phone.
"These apps come here also," he said, and showed us three forwarded messages from his WhatsApp, each one inviting him to "earn daily from your phone."
One promised daily income from "AI trading".
One promised daily income for "watching ads".
One promised daily income for "investing in a solar project".
The names were different, but the shape was the same as SD Pay: a deposit, a daily credit inside the app, and extra income for bringing people.
Munna said he had never joined any of them, and when I asked why, he pointed at his kettle.
"This kettle gives me income every day," he said. "But I have to stand here and make tea. If the phone gives me income without standing, then who is standing for me?"
I have met founders with expensive degrees who do not understand money as clearly as Munna does.
His stall, it turns out, could use some simple help, and that is part of why Kaka and I are here.
He takes most of his payments through UPI now, and he has no idea what his monthly sales really are, because the money comes in small amounts all day.
I showed him how to read his own [UPI transaction history as a simple sales report](https://ethicalfounder.in/kenya-fastagger-upi-transaction-history-sales-report-shop-owners/), and he was astonished at how much tea he sells on festival days compared to ordinary days.
That is the kind of automation small shops need: nothing grand, only a clear picture of their own money, so that the next app promising daily income has to compete with a number the shopkeeper already trusts.
We plan to sit with Munna again in the coming days and set up a small daily summary for him, the kind I described in [daily business reporting automation](https://ethicalfounder.in/daily-business-reporting-automation/).
He said he would pay us in tea, and Kaka said that was the best rate he had ever been offered.

## Where AI enters an app based investment scheme

Munna's first forwarded message, the one promising daily income from "AI trading", stayed with me after we left his stall.
On the drive back, I asked Kaka how often he hears the word AI from the shopkeepers he knows in Kashi.
"Every week," he said. "Nobody knows what it means, but everybody knows it means money."
That is the new layer on these old schemes.
The structure of SD Pay and its copies is as old as chit-fund collapses, but the language around them now borrows heavily from artificial intelligence.
An app says its returns come from an AI engine, a trading algorithm or a smart arbitrage system, and the shopkeeper, who has heard on television that AI is changing everything, accepts that explanation.
I have tracked how crowded and noisy the AI space has become in my note on [AI agents and market saturation](https://ethicalfounder.in/ai-agents-market-saturation-2026/), and the noise itself has become a cover for fraud.
The promotional videos for these apps now often feature presenters who may not be real people at all, and I would encourage every reader to learn [how to check whether an image or video is AI generated](https://ethicalfounder.in/check-if-image-is-ai-generated-synthid-detector/).
Many of these apps also run their "customer support" through automated WhatsApp chats that reply instantly and politely and never answer the actual question, which is why it helps to understand [what the rules say about WhatsApp AI chatbots](https://ethicalfounder.in/whatsapp-ai-chatbot-rules-2026/) and what a genuine business bot is allowed to do.
The honest use of AI in a small shop looks very different.
It is a reminder to a customer who owes money, a summary of the day's sales, an automatic reply that tells a customer the shop's timings.
I have described many such uses in [easy business automation for Indian MSMEs](https://ethicalfounder.in/easy-business-automation-for-indian-msme/), and none of them promise anyone a daily percentage.
Kaka put it in his own way as we turned into his lane.
"Real AI helps you do your work," he said. "Fake AI tells you that you don't need to work."
For a shopkeeper weighing small new costs, such as the charges that may come on [UPI payments for small shops](https://ethicalfounder.in/upi-mdr-charges-small-shops-p2pm-exemption/), the real tools save money, and the fake ones take all of it.
Before any app that mentions AI touches your money, run the same registered investment platform check you would run for any other investment, because a word on a screen is not a licence.

## Six questions for a registered investment platform check

On the walk back to the car, Hasmukhbhai asked me for a list he could print and put near his shop counter.
I gave him these six questions, and I am repeating them here for every shopkeeper and every customer.
First, who is the regulated company behind this app, and what is its registration?
A real investment product in India is linked to a bank, a SEBI-registered intermediary, an RBI-regulated entity or a government scheme, and its registration can be checked.
Second, where does my money go when I deposit it?
If the money goes to a personal UPI ID, an individual's bank account, or a chain of different accounts, stop there.
Third, is the return fixed and daily?
No regulated product guarantees a fixed daily return, so this answer alone is enough to walk away.
Fourth, does my income increase when I bring others?
A referral structure that pays you from other people's deposits is the core of a money circulation scheme.
Fifth, can I find this company's name in official records and its office at a real address?
Search the company on the [RBI's Sachet portal](https://sachet.rbi.org.in/), which also allows the public to report entities taking deposits without permission, and look up its registration details wherever it claims to be registered.
Sixth, what happens if I want all my money back tomorrow?
Ask this before you invest, and watch the reaction carefully.
A registered investment platform check takes perhaps twenty minutes, and those twenty minutes are cheaper than any loss.
Kaka suggested adding a seventh line at the bottom of the printed sheet, in Gujarati, so that customers would read it twice.
"If a stranger offers you a share of Lakshmi without work, ask him why he is not keeping it for himself."
Hasmukhbhai said he would ask the local printer to make it in large letters.

## What the law says about a money circulation scheme

India has clear laws against these schemes, and it helps small traders to know that the law is on the side of those who refuse.
The Prize Chits and Money Circulation Schemes (Banning) Act prohibits schemes where people pay money to join and earn mainly by enrolling others.
The Banning of Unregulated Deposit Schemes Act makes it illegal to take deposits from the public without proper regulatory approval, and it gives investors a stronger path to complain and to have assets attached.
State laws on the protection of depositors give the police and the courts powers to seize property linked to such schemes and to use it for refunds.
None of this works quickly.
Hasmukhbhai's nephew and his friends will likely wait a long time, and they may recover only part of what they lost.
But knowing these laws matters in two ways.
It tells the honest shopkeeper that he can refuse to promote any app based investment scheme without fear of being called old-fashioned.
And it tells the person who has already lost money that he is a complainant with rights, not someone to be ashamed.
For readers who run small businesses and who feel tempted by such income because their own earnings are thin, I would gently suggest looking at steady options instead, such as the ones I discuss in [business ideas for small towns](https://ethicalfounder.in/business-ideas-for-small-towns/) or the honest arithmetic of [a side income compared to a registered business](https://ethicalfounder.in/side-income-vs-registered-business/).
They are slower.
They are also yours.

## When the investment app withdrawal problem begins

Kaka drove us back slowly through the narrow lanes, and in the car Hasmukhbhai asked what his nephew should do now.
I gave him the same order of steps that I give every reader in this situation, adjusted for the SD Pay app fraud case.
Stop putting any more money in, including any "unlock fee", "tax fee" or "upgrade fee" to release your wallet.
Take screenshots of the investment app wallet, every deposit, every UPI transaction ID, and every chat with the person who introduced you.
Write a timeline in a notebook: when you joined, through whom, how much, to which account, and when withdrawals stopped.
Join the complaint that the district police are already handling rather than filing a separate one in a different station, and ask for a receipt.
Speak openly with the friends you brought in, and if possible, go to the police together.
Keep your shop's daily books clean and separate from this loss, so that the business itself is not dragged down with it.
If your business has borrowed money because of the app, speak to your bank early instead of hiding it; banks are far more flexible with honest borrowers who come early than with those who come late.
I have written about [bank loans for small business ideas](https://ethicalfounder.in/business-ideas-that-need-a-bank-loan/), and the same principle holds in difficult times: a bank relationship is built on honesty, and that honesty is easier to keep than to rebuild.
Hasmukhbhai wrote it all down in his small red diary, the kind traders use for credit accounts.
He said he would read it to his nephew on the phone that night, and then, after a pause, said he would also tell him to come to Kashi one day, "so he can sit on the river and understand that money is not the worst thing a man can lose."

## The river, the fire and the money we carry

Before we dropped Hasmukhbhai at the dharmashala, Kaka stopped the car for a few minutes near a small Shiva temple in a lane where an old priest was closing the doors for the night.
We stood outside and folded our hands, and the priest, who knew Kaka, came out and put a little vibhuti on each of our foreheads.
Hasmukhbhai asked him, half joking, whether Bholenath forgives people who lose money to greed.
The priest smiled and said that Shiva forgives everyone who comes to him with an honest heart, but that forgiveness does not return the money, it returns the mind.
I have been thinking about that line since last night.
In the karma view I grew up with, every action has its fruit, and some fruits are bitter.
The nephew acted out of hope, and the fruit was a loss.
The people who designed the app acted out of greed and deception, and their fruit will come in its own time, in court and beyond it.
But there is a third action in every such story, which belongs to the rest of us: the action of warning, of asking questions, of refusing to pass the message on.
That action is small, and its fruit is invisible, because it is a loss that never happens.
I think that is the best kind of fruit there is.
Sitting on Bhola's boat, watching the fires of Harishchandra Ghat, I realised that every person who reaches that bank leaves behind every app, every wallet and every daily credit.
What goes with them is only what they did.
An investment app can show you a balance every morning.
It cannot show you that.
The next story of this journey came the following day, at a small agarbatti workshop on the edge of the city that Kaka wanted me to see, from a visitor from Patna who had a cousin, a contract and a mobile tower that never stood.
I will write it after I have had a little more sleep.

## Questions People Ask About Investment App

**What is the SD Pay app fraud case?**

According to Gujarati and Hindi reports, Amreli police have alleged that the SD Pay app, also called Apna Pay, collected money from lakhs of investors with promises of daily returns and referral bonuses before shutting down, and two alleged main operators were arrested in Odisha.

**Can any investment app in India promise daily income?**

No regulated investment product in India offers a guaranteed fixed daily return, so an app that promises one should be treated as a serious warning sign.

**What is a daily income investment plan?**

It is an offer that credits a fixed small percentage to your app wallet every day on the money you deposit, which looks small daily but adds up to impossible annual returns.

**How do I check whether an investment platform is registered?**

Ask for its registration with RBI, SEBI or another regulator, verify it on the regulator's own website, confirm the company's address and directors, and use RBI's Sachet portal to check or report unauthorised deposit-taking.

**Why do these schemes spread through shopkeepers?**

Shopkeepers are trusted by many customers every day, so when a shopkeeper joins and shows his daily credits, customers follow him, which is why shopkeeper investment fraud spreads quickly.

**What is a money circulation scheme?**

It is a scheme in which people pay to join and earn mainly by bringing in new members, and it is banned in India under the Prize Chits and Money Circulation Schemes (Banning) Act.

**What should I do if I face an investment app withdrawal problem?**

Stop depositing, refuse any unlock fee, save screenshots and transaction IDs, write a timeline, and join or file a police complaint, preferably together with other affected investors.

**Is a referral income app always a scam?**

Not always, since some genuine apps pay small referral rewards, but when your income depends mainly on other people's deposits rather than on a product or service, it works as a money circulation scheme.

**What did the Amreli police investment case reveal?**

It showed how an app based investment scheme can reach lakhs of people through shops, families and referral chains, and how quickly it can collapse when new deposits slow down.

**How can a small shop protect its customers from such apps?**

Display a short list of checks at the counter, refuse to promote any daily income app, and help customers see that a regulated product never guarantees daily returns.
