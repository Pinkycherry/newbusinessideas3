# Counterfeit packaging now takes minutes with AI, and the honest trader pays for it, as one Varanasi jeweller learnt the hard way

## The box that came back

Subramanya Sharma keeps his imitation jewellery shop in a lane so narrow that two scooters cannot pass each other, and he likes it that way. "People who come here have walked for it," he told me the first time Shankar Kaka took me there. "They are not passing by. They are coming to me."

[IMAGE 1]

He is from Andhra, like Kaka and me, and he came to Varanasi many years ago with two suitcases of bangles and a promise to his mother that he would send money home every month. He kept that promise. The shop is now a small, bright room with glass cases of kundan sets, oxidised earrings, temple-style necklaces and bridal bangles in every colour a wedding season can ask for.

The afternoon I am writing about, a few hours before I sat down with this notebook on the ghat steps, Subramanya was not smiling.

On his counter sat a necklace box. It looked exactly like his own. Same maroon velvet, same gold-foil border, same small lotus he had designed with a printer in the city years ago, same line in Hindi that says the piece is made with care in Kashi. "This is not mine," he said, and opened it.

Inside was a necklace so light that it bent when he lifted it, the gold colour already rubbing off at one corner.

A customer had brought it back that morning, furious, saying she had bought it as a gift from "Sharma ji's shop" and that it had turned black in a week. She had not bought it from him.

She had bought it from a stall near the station that was selling cheap pieces in boxes that copied his. The counterfeit packaging had done what a counterfeiter always wants it to do: it had borrowed his name and left him the complaint.

## What the Meerut raid showed about duplicate packaging box factories

That same evening, Kaka found a report on his phone and passed it across to me while Subramanya was still turning the fake box over in his hands.

[IMAGE 2]

It was a Hindi report from Meerut, on [Dainik Bhaskar's Meerut page](https://www.bhaskar.com/local/uttar-pradesh/meerut/video/meerut-fake-branded-packaging-box-factory-busted-police-raid-139584433.html), about a raid on a unit that was preparing duplicate packaging boxes of well-known companies for branded electronic goods ahead of the festival season.

According to the report, the raid was carried out with the police after a tip-off, and the idea was simple: packaging that looked exactly like the real thing, with cheaper goods to be filled inside and pushed into the market.

A second report from Patna, which Kaka had also seen that week, described police and company officials raiding a house in Fatuha where packaging of a well-known edible oil brand was allegedly being copied. Two cities, two very different products, the same method.

A duplicate packaging box is cheap to make and expensive to detect, and a packaging design copy can now be produced from a single photograph. The goods inside can be anything. What sells them is the box.

Subramanya listened to all of this without speaking, and then said something that made Kaka put the phone down. "For electronics it is the big company that loses," he said. "For me, it is my name. I am the brand. If the box lies, my face lies."

## How fake labels made with AI sped up the counterfeiter

I asked Subramanya how he thought someone had copied his box so perfectly. He had a theory, and it turned out to be right. A young man had visited his shop a few weeks earlier, asked many questions about prices and photographed the display "for his sister's wedding". Subramanya now believes those photographs were enough.

[IMAGE 3]

Ten years ago, copying a box design meant finding a skilled designer, recreating the artwork by hand, matching colours at the printer and paying for trial runs.

Today, a clear photograph can be turned into print-ready artwork in an afternoon with image tools that clean up the lines, rebuild the lettering, match the colours and even generate a convincing lotus where the photograph was blurred. Fake labels made with AI are no longer clumsy, and a careless stockist cannot tell them apart from the real thing.

The spelling is correct, the fonts are close, the foil effect is simulated well enough to pass on a crowded stall under a yellow bulb.

I have written before about how sellers use [AI product images for Amazon and Flipkart](https://ethicalfounder.in/ai-product-images-for-amazon-and-flipkart/), and every one of those honest uses has a dishonest twin. The same tool that helps a small seller make a beautiful catalogue helps a counterfeiter make a beautiful lie.

India has begun to address synthetic content through [AI labelling rules under the IT Rules amendment](https://ethicalfounder.in/ai-labelling-rules-in-india-it-rules-amendment/), but a printed box on a station stall carries no digital label at all. Packaging design copy has become a home-printer job, and that is the new reality every small brand has to plan for.

## Who loses when counterfeit packaging spreads

Kaka, who has been a guide in Kashi long enough to have opinions about every lane, put it in terms of pilgrims. "A pilgrim buys one thing to take home," he said.

"If it breaks, he does not remember the stall. He remembers Kashi." That is the reach of counterfeit packaging. It hurts more people than the one who bought the fake.

### The buyer who trusted the box

The woman who returned the necklace was not careless. She recognised a box she had seen in her cousin's house, a box she associated with good quality, and she paid a little more than the stall's other pieces because of it. Duplicate products in market stalls depend on exactly this recognition. The buyer does the counterfeiter's marketing for him.

### The shopkeeper who stocks the fake without knowing

The stall near the station may not have made the boxes either. Subramanya suspects the stall owner bought a bundle of "packed sets" from a wholesaler at a price that should have raised questions and did not. Many small stockists across India sell duplicate products in market lanes without knowing, because the box looks right and the margin looks good.

When a complaint comes, the stockist is the first person blamed and often the last person who knew.

### The brand that made the real one

And then there is Subramanya himself. His loss is not only one returned necklace. It is every customer who now hesitates before buying a box with his lotus on it, every wedding family that will ask "is this the real Sharma ji box?", every rupee he will now spend on proving that he is himself. "My father sold bangles from a basket," he said.

"He never needed a box. Now I have a box and I need to protect it from thieves."

## Jay Kishori's bhajan and the price of a name

That night, Kaka insisted we go to the ghats for the evening aarti, and on the way back we found a small crowd around a devotional programme where Jay Kishori was singing.

I had heard her many times on recordings, and hearing her voice carry over the water in person was one of the gifts of this journey. After the programme, we were fortunate to meet her briefly, and Kaka, who never misses a chance, asked for a selfie for his mother. She was gracious and patient with everyone who came to her, and she spoke only about devotion and the city.

I mention her here for one reason. Walking back, Subramanya, who had come with us, said that people trust a familiar voice the way they trust a familiar box. "If someone copied her voice and sold it in a stall," he said, "everyone would know it was false within one line. Why can't people know my box is false?"

Because a voice carries a soul and a box does not, Kaka replied, and then added that this is exactly why the soul of a business has to be built into things the counterfeiter cannot copy. That idea shaped everything we did with Subramanya over the next two days.

## How to identify fake products at the counter with genuine product verification

The next morning we sat in Subramanya's shop with tea and the two boxes, his real one and the fake, side by side.

[IMAGE 4]

We made a list of every difference, and then turned that list into a simple check that any stockist or buyer can do. This is the table we wrote together, and Subramanya has since printed it and pinned it behind his counter.

| What to check | Genuine packaging usually shows | Counterfeit packaging often shows |
|---|---|---|
| Weight of the product inside | Consistent weight for the same design | Noticeably lighter pieces in the same box |
| Print sharpness on small text | Clean edges on fine lettering and borders | Slightly blurred or thickened fine text |
| Foil and embossing | Raised foil you can feel with a fingertip | Flat print imitating foil, no texture |
| Batch or design code | A code that matches the seller's records | Missing code, or the same code on every box |
| Inner lining and finishing | Even glue lines, aligned lining | Uneven glue, lining cut slightly off |
| Seller's contact details | Phone and address that answer and match | Old or wrong number, or no address |
| Price | Close to the brand's normal range | Much lower, or oddly higher "for the box" |

How to identify fake products comes down to slowing down for thirty seconds. Lift it, feel it, read the small text, check the code and call the number if you are unsure. Genuine product verification, as Subramanya now tells every reseller, is mostly a habit of paying attention, and the counterfeiter is betting that you will not.

## Brand protection for small business, built into the box

Subramanya's real question was not how to spot fakes. It was how to make his own boxes harder to copy without doubling his costs, because his margins are honest and thin. We worked through it slowly, using what a small jeweller can actually afford.

First, a numbered card inside every box, signed by hand, with the design name and a short serial that he records in a notebook and now in a simple shared sheet on his shop phone.

A customer who doubts a piece can send the serial on WhatsApp and get an instant reply confirming it, which is the kind of small automation I describe in [easy business automation for Indian MSMEs](https://ethicalfounder.in/easy-business-automation-for-indian-msme/).

Second, one physical detail that is tedious to copy: a thread tie in a specific colour pattern that changes each season.

Third, a printed line on every bill telling the customer that genuine pieces come only from his shop and his two named resellers, with their addresses. Fourth, regular photographs of his own boxes and pieces in a dated folder, so that if he ever needs to prove the design is his, he has a record. None of these stop a determined counterfeiter. All of them make the fake easier to catch and the real one easier to trust.

Brand protection for small business is rarely about one expensive technology. It is about many small, consistent signs that only the real seller bothers to keep.

## Trademark protection India offers a small brand

Subramanya had never registered his lotus mark. Like many small traders, he assumed trademarks were for big companies.

[IMAGE 5]

I explained that trademark protection india provides is open to individuals and small businesses, that the application can be filed online through the [official Intellectual Property India portal](https://ipindia.gov.in/), and that small entities and startups pay lower government fees than large companies.

A registered mark gives him a much stronger footing when he sends a notice to a wholesaler, files a police complaint, or asks an online marketplace to take down a copied listing.

His box design itself may also be protected as a design registration if it is original and new, and the printed artwork may carry copyright. I told him plainly that registration takes time and that he should file early, because a mark registered after a counterfeit has spread is harder to defend than one registered before.

For founders weighing whether this is worth it, the question is simple: if your name is the reason customers choose you, the name is an asset, and assets are protected before they are stolen. I have written about how [a small business stops doing everything itself](https://ethicalfounder.in/when-a-small-business-owner-stops-doing-everything/), and legal protection is one of those jobs where a professional is worth the fee.

## Filing a counterfeit goods complaint that gets attention

We also talked about what Subramanya could do now about the stall near the station. A counterfeit goods complaint works best when it is specific and documented.

He should collect the fake box and the piece, the bill if the customer has one, photographs of the stall's display with the date, and his own design records showing the box has been his for years.

He can then send a written notice to the stall owner, and if the copying continues, file a complaint with the local police under the relevant provisions for cheating and trademark or copyright infringement, attaching all of this.

If a wholesaler is supplying the boxes, the complaint should name the supply chain as far as he can trace it, because the stall is often the smallest link.

The Meerut and Patna raids both started with information reaching the police and the brand owners, and a small seller's information can start the same chain. Kaka offered to walk with him to the stall owner first, before any police complaint.

"Many of these stall men are also poor," he said. "Sometimes they will stop when they learn whose name they are using. Sometimes they will tell you who sold them the boxes." That is a good order of steps for any small trader: speak first, document always, escalate when needed.

## Duplicate products in market stalls and the walk to the station

On the second morning, Kaka, Subramanya and I walked to the stall near the station where the fake necklace had been sold. Kaka insisted we go before any complaint was written, and he did the talking, in the easy Bhojpuri he uses with everyone in the city.

The stall owner was a thin young man named Raju with a wife and a small son helping him sort earrings into plastic trays, and when he saw the two maroon boxes side by side, his face went pale.

He had bought a bundle of two hundred packed sets from a wholesaler in the old market three weeks earlier, at a price he admitted was "very good". He had no idea whose lotus was on the lid. "I thought it was a company design," he said. "Every box has a design." That sentence explains how duplicate products in market stalls move so freely.

The stockist sees a nice box, the buyer sees a familiar box, and only the original maker sees his own name being sold back to him. Subramanya looked at Raju's son for a long moment and then did something I did not expect.

He offered to buy back the remaining fake boxes at cost, on one condition: that Raju give him the wholesaler's name and shop address in writing, and that he stock only genuine pieces from then on, which Subramanya would supply on fair credit. Raju agreed on the spot. Kaka told me later that in Kashi this is called turning a thief's road into a pilgrim's path. I call it smart business.

A fake stockist turned into a real reseller is worth more to Subramanya than any notice, and the wholesaler's name is now a lead for the police if the copying continues.

The packaging design copy had started with one photograph and a careless wholesaler. It ended, at least at this stall, with a handshake and a written address.

Walking back along the river, Kaka said that every lane in Kashi has a Raju, a man who sells what he is given without asking where it came from, and that most of them would choose the honest road if someone showed them one that still fed their family.

I think that is true of most small stockists across India, and it is why a brand owner's first conversation with a stall should be an offer before it becomes a threat.

## What one fake box costs a small brand

That afternoon, Subramanya asked me to help him put a number on the damage, because, as he said, "a loss you cannot count is a loss you cannot fight." We sat with his sales notebook and his UPI history, which he now reads as a daily sales record using the method I described for [reading UPI transaction history as a sales report](https://ethicalfounder.in/kenya-fastagger-upi-transaction-history-sales-report-shop-owners/).

His festival-week sales were lower than the same week the year before, even though footfall in the lane had been higher. Two regular wedding-season families had not returned.

One reseller had asked, without saying why, whether his boxes had "changed". None of this proves that the counterfeit packaging caused every rupee of the drop, and I told him so. But it shows the shape of the harm: fewer repeat customers, more questions, more time spent proving himself.

For a small brand, reputation is the main marketing budget, which is why I keep repeating that [word of mouth beats any advertisement](https://ethicalfounder.in/word-of-mouth-vs-ads-for-a-first-year-business/) in the early years.

When the word of mouth turns into "be careful with that box", the brand has to work twice as hard.

We set up a simple daily record for him, sales by design and by reseller, the same kind of note I describe in [daily business reporting](https://ethicalfounder.in/daily-business-reporting-automation/), so that the next time a fake appears he will see the drop within days, not months.

We also drafted a short message his staff can send to any customer who calls with a doubt, with the serial check and the list of genuine resellers, the kind of reply I set out in [customer service automation for small businesses](https://ethicalfounder.in/customer-service-automation/). Brand protection for small business, in the end, is as much about records and replies as about lawyers.

## Why Subramanya now wants to manufacture

The most interesting turn of this story came at the end of the second day. Subramanya told us he has been thinking for months about moving from reselling imitation jewellery made by others to manufacturing a small range of his own, with two or three artisans in a rented room near his house. The counterfeit episode had made up his mind.

"If I make it myself," he said, "I control the metal, the finish, the box, everything. Nobody can sell my piece without my hands in it." I told him that manufacturing is a serious step and that his numbers have to be clear before he takes it: material costs, artisan wages, wastage, the time to build stock, and how long wedding-season customers take to pay resellers.

We sat with his notebook and drew a first version of those numbers, the same way I approach the margins in [the small-town pharmacy lesson on retail margins](https://ethicalfounder.in/small-town-pharmacy-business-lessons-retail-margins/).

He will also need a simple record for each batch, artisan by artisan, so that every piece can be traced, which is where a little automation will save him hours. If he needs capital, a bank loan against a clear plan is the honest route, and I pointed him to my notes on [business ideas that need a bank loan](https://ethicalfounder.in/business-ideas-that-need-a-bank-loan/).

Kaka and I have promised to help him set up the batch records and the serial check before the next wedding season.

It is exactly the kind of opportunity this journey is looking for: a real trader, a real problem, and a way forward that makes his business stronger instead of more fearful.

## What the counterfeiter can never copy

On our last evening with him, Subramanya closed the shop early and came with us to the river.

[IMAGE 6]

He bought a small marigold garland from a flower seller on the steps and offered it at a little Shiva shrine near the water, and then sat beside us on the stone, quiet for a long time.

"My father used to say that Bholenath sees the inside of every box," he said finally. Kaka smiled and said that this is why Shiva is called the lord who sees through every disguise.

In my own understanding of karma, the counterfeiter takes a borrowed name and gives back a broken promise, and the fruit of that action will find him in its own time. But the honest seller's karma is not only to suffer the copy.

It is to keep making the real thing better, to keep his word on every bill, and to build into his work the things a thief cannot fake: his care, his traceable records, his relationship with his customers. A counterfeit packaging factory can print a lotus.

It cannot print the years Subramanya has spent answering his phone when a customer calls with a problem. For any small brand reading this, that is where protection really begins.

## Questions People Ask About Counterfeit Packaging

**What is counterfeit packaging?**

Counterfeit packaging is a box, label or wrapper made to copy a real brand's design so that cheaper or fake goods inside can be sold as genuine.

**How do I identify fake products from their packaging?**

Check the weight of the product, the sharpness of small text, raised foil or embossing, batch codes, the inner finishing and whether the seller's contact details answer and match.

**Are fake labels made with AI harder to spot?**

They are often cleaner and closer to the original than older fakes, so buyers should rely on physical checks such as weight, texture and serial codes rather than the print alone.

**How can a small business protect its packaging design from copies?**

Use a serial card inside each box, one tedious physical detail such as a coloured thread tie, a printed line on every bill naming genuine sellers, dated design records, and register your trademark and design.

**Is trademark protection in India available for small businesses?**

Yes, individuals and small businesses can apply online through the Intellectual Property India portal, and small entities pay lower government fees than large companies.

**What should I include in a counterfeit goods complaint?**

Include the fake item and box, the buyer's bill if available, dated photographs of where it was sold, your design records, and any information about the wholesaler or supplier.

**Is selling duplicate products in market stalls a crime even if the seller did not make them?**

Knowingly selling goods with a copied trademark can be an offence, and a stockist who continues after being informed takes on serious legal risk.

**What is a duplicate packaging box racket?**

It is an operation that prints boxes copying a brand's packaging so that low-quality goods can be filled inside and sold as the real product, like the unit reported raided in Meerut.

**How does genuine product verification work for a small jeweller?**

The jeweller records a serial for each piece, places a signed card in the box, and confirms the serial instantly when a customer sends it, so fakes without a matching serial are exposed.

**Can AI tools be used to copy a packaging design from a photo?**

Yes, a clear photograph can now be turned into print-ready artwork quickly, which is why small brands should rely on physical and record-based protection as well as legal registration.
