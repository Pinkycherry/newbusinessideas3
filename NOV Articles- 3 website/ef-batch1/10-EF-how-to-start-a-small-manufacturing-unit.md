# How to start a small manufacturing unit in India with a government loan, learnt from five makers we met between Kashi and a village farm

## The question Subramanya asked on the ghat steps

The evening after we finished his customer system, Subramanya Sharma sat with Shankar Kaka and me on the steps near Harishchandra Ghat and asked the question he had been carrying for months. "I have sold other people's jewellery for many years," he said. "When do I start making my own?"

It is the question that separates a trader from a maker, and it is one of the most important decisions a small business owner in India can take. Trading earns a margin on someone else's product. Manufacturing earns a margin on your own skill, your own design and your own control of quality, and it builds an asset that can grow, be financed by a bank and one day be passed on.

[IMAGE 1]

Kaka, who knows half of Varanasi's small workshops, did not answer the question directly. Instead, he made a plan. "Before you start one unit," he said to Subramanya, "come with us and see four others. Then you will know what to ask." Over the next several days, we visited an incense maker, a snack seller with a dream, a strategist's village farm and, by video, a fastener manufacturer in Mumbai.

This article is what we learnt about how to start a small manufacturing unit in India, with five real product ideas, the government support that exists, the registrations and licences, the numbers to check and the mistakes to avoid. It is long, because the decision is serious. I have written it in the order we lived it.

## Why a small manufacturing unit, and why now

Small manufacturing is one of the quiet strengths of India's economy. Most of it happens in units with a handful of workers, in rented sheds, back rooms and village workshops, making things that larger factories either cannot or do not want to make in small batches. Many of these units supply local markets, temple towns, district wholesalers and, increasingly, online buyers across the country.

Three changes make this a good time for a small manufacturing unit. First, registration has become simpler and free through Udyam, which gives a unit an official MSME identity in one online process. Second, government-backed credit for new units, including subsidy-linked loans for first-time entrepreneurs, is available through banks across the country. Third, digital tools have made it far easier for a small maker to reach buyers directly, keep records and present a professional face, as I explained in [how sellers use AI product images on marketplaces](https://ethicalfounder.in/ai-product-images-for-amazon-and-flipkart/).

There is also a cultural shift. Many young Indians who once wanted only salaried jobs now want to build something of their own, and many families who once trusted only land and gold are now willing to back a son's or daughter's workshop. Kaka put it in his own words: "Earlier the village sent its boys to the city to work in factories. Now some of them are coming back to start factories."

I am careful not to romanticise this. Manufacturing is harder than trading, the cash cycle is longer, and mistakes in machinery or licences can be expensive. But for the right person with the right product, a small unit can be the most stable business they ever build.

## Idea one: imitation jewellery manufacturing, done the modern way

We began with Subramanya's own field, because his question was the reason for the journey. Imitation jewellery manufacturing in India is a large, fragmented industry, with clusters in several cities producing for weddings, festivals and daily wear. Subramanya's advantage is that he already knows the buyers, the designs that sell in Kashi and the price points his resellers can manage.

His plan is modest and sensible: a rented room near his house, two or three skilled artisans, a small polishing and plating setup, and a limited range of temple-style and oxidised designs that his existing customers already ask for. He would start by making perhaps a third of what he sells, keep buying the rest from suppliers, and grow the share of his own production as quality and demand allow.

[IMAGE 2]

The modern part is not the metal. It is the system around it. Each batch is recorded with the artisan, the date, the design and the quantity, so that every piece can be traced, and a small serial card goes in every box. Designs are photographed and recorded with dates to protect his work, and his customer records, which we had just set up, tell him which designs to make before each wedding season.

The questions he must answer before spending are the same for every small manufacturing unit. What is the material cost per piece, including wastage? What is the artisan cost per piece? How many pieces can the room produce in a month? How long will it take to sell a month's production? And how long will resellers take to pay? We worked through first versions of these numbers on his counter, and I will return to them in the section on money.

## Idea two: a floral waste recycling business from temple flowers

The second visit was to Dinesh Gupta's incense workshop on the edge of the city, and it produced the idea that excited Kaka most. Every day, the temples and ghats of Kashi receive enormous quantities of flowers as offerings, marigolds, roses, lotus and others, and much of it ends up in the river or in waste heaps.

Dinesh has been experimenting quietly with a floral waste recycling business. Flowers collected from temples are sorted, dried and ground, then mixed into incense cones and sticks, while some petals are used to make natural colours and others go into compost. In several Indian cities, small enterprises have turned temple flowers into incense, natural dyes, handmade paper and compost, and the idea fits Kashi almost perfectly.

[IMAGE 3]

"The flowers were offered with devotion," Dinesh said. "It feels wrong to throw them away. If they become incense again, the devotion returns to the temple." Kaka was moved by this and said that it is the most Kashi idea he had ever heard: nothing offered to Mahadev should go to waste.

From a business view, the floral waste recycling business has strong advantages. The raw material is collected rather than bought, though collection, sorting and drying take labour and space. The story is powerful, which helps with buyers and with premium pricing. And it can be started small, alongside an existing incense unit, without large machinery at first.

The challenges are real too. Collection needs reliable arrangements with temples and their committees, drying needs space and good weather or simple dryers, and quality must be consistent, because a buyer who pays more for a story expects the product to be excellent. Dinesh plans to start with one temple's flowers, test the cones with his existing buyers, and expand only when the process is steady.

## Idea three: millet snack manufacturing from Sudarshini's kitchen

The third visit was the most personal. Sudarshini aunty, the snack seller from the evening gathering on the ghats, invited us to her small home to taste the snacks she sells and to talk about her dream of a family business with her husband. She makes crisp, savoury snacks that people eat on the steps at dusk, and she had been experimenting with versions made from millets.

Millet snack manufacturing is one of the most promising food categories for small units in India right now. Millets have become popular with health-conscious buyers, and government programmes have promoted them widely. A small unit making millet-based namkeen, ready mixes or baked snacks can sell locally through shops and temple roads, and later through distributors and online channels.

[IMAGE 4]

Sudarshini's plan is careful. She wants to start in a rented space with a commercial kitchen setup, a fryer or roaster, a small packing machine and two helpers, and to sell first through the shops near the ghats that already know her. Her husband would handle purchasing and accounts, and their daughter, when she is older, would manage orders. She does not want to borrow to start. She wants to borrow to grow, once she has proved demand.

For food manufacturing, the registrations are more demanding. An FSSAI registration or licence is mandatory, depending on the scale, and the kitchen must meet hygiene standards. Labels must carry the required information, including ingredients and best-before dates. I told Sudarshini that this is not a reason to delay, but a reason to plan properly, and that a clean, well-labelled packet is itself a mark of quality for a buyer.

She asked whether she could start from her home kitchen. For very small, registered home food businesses, that is possible in many cases, but once she employs helpers and sells in volume, a separate production space is wiser for hygiene, insurance and family peace. "My daughter should study in the house," she said, "not breathe oil all day." That settled it.

## Idea four: a vegetable powder processing unit on a village farm

The fourth visit took us back to Sahadev's village, about eighty kilometres from Varanasi, where the IIT-trained strategist is preparing his own manufacturing venture on his family's land. His idea is a vegetable powder processing unit: drying and grinding locally grown vegetables, herbs and leaves into powders that food businesses, restaurants and households use.

The logic is strong. Farmers in his area often sell surplus vegetables at very low prices in the peak season, or watch them spoil. A drying and powdering unit can buy that surplus at a fair price, turn it into a product with a long shelf life, and sell it through the year. Powders of tomato, onion, moringa, beetroot, ginger and similar crops are used widely in food processing and home cooking.

[IMAGE 5]

Sahadev's plan includes solar-assisted or electric dryers, a pulveriser, sieving and packing equipment, and a small quality-testing setup for moisture. He has costed every machine, estimated the local supply from farmers he knows personally, and written a monthly cash plan for the first two years. He is applying for a government-backed loan for the machinery and working capital, and his project report is one of the most thorough I have seen.

I asked him why he did not simply take a corporate job again. He laughed and pointed at the fields. "In the city I made reports about companies," he said. "Here, I can make a company." His mother, listening from the courtyard, said she would believe it when the first packet was on her kitchen shelf.

This idea is a strong fit for anyone with access to farm produce and some technical ability. It also connects agriculture to manufacturing, which is where many of India's best rural opportunities lie. The challenges are consistency of supply, drying quality, food safety compliance and building buyer relationships beyond the first few customers.

## Idea five: ancillary manufacturing for local industry, like Sudhanshu's unit

The fifth example came by video call from Mumbai, where Sahadev's elder brother Sudhanshu runs a small fastener manufacturing unit. His business makes nuts, bolts and related parts for local industries, and it represents a different kind of small manufacturing unit: ancillary manufacturing, where a small unit supplies parts or components to larger businesses.

Ancillary units have a particular strength. They sell to businesses that need regular supplies, so demand can be steady once relationships are built. They can grow by adding machines and customers gradually. And in industrial areas, there is usually a cluster of similar units, suppliers and skilled workers nearby, which reduces the cost of starting.

[IMAGE 6]

Sudhanshu's advice to Subramanya, after hearing his plan, was the most practical of the whole journey. "Make one thing very well before you make ten things," he said. "My first customer stayed with me because my bolts were always the right size, not because they were cheap." He also warned that industrial buyers often pay late, and that a small manufacturer must plan for that from the first day.

The lesson applies beyond fasteners. Many small manufacturing business ideas India offers are ancillary in nature: packaging for food units, parts for machinery makers, components for electrical goods, labels and printed material for other manufacturers. A unit that becomes reliable for a few larger buyers can build a stable business without any consumer marketing at all.

## Udyam registration for manufacturers, the first official step

Whatever the product, the first official step for a small manufacturing unit in India is usually Udyam registration. Udyam registration for manufacturers is free, done online on the [official Udyam Registration portal](https://udyamregistration.gov.in/), and based on the owner's Aadhaar and PAN. It classifies the business as micro, small or medium based on investment and turnover, and gives it an official identity as an MSME.

The registration itself does not give money. What it does is open doors. Many government schemes, priority sector lending from banks, protection against delayed payments from buyers under MSME rules, and various state incentives require or prefer an Udyam-registered unit. I advise every new maker to register as soon as the business has a clear name and address.

Sudarshini asked whether there was any fee. There is none on the official portal, and I warned her strongly against websites that charge for "Udyam registration services," because the official process is free and simple. Kaka promised to sit with her and do it on his phone in one evening.

Alongside Udyam, most units will also need GST registration once turnover crosses the threshold or when selling to businesses that need GST invoices, and many register early to look professional to buyers. For readers unsure how their tax identity changes when they move from freelancing or trading to running a unit, I have explained [the tax identity difference between freelancers and business owners](https://ethicalfounder.in/freelancer-vs-business-owner-tax-identity-difference/), and for very small units, [presumptive taxation under Section 58](https://ethicalfounder.in/section-58-income-tax-act-2025-presumptive-tax-freelancers/) may be relevant.

## PMEGP loan for manufacturing and the other government routes

The question everyone at Kaka's house wanted answered was about money. The government offers several routes for new small manufacturing units, and understanding them clearly is half the battle. The PMEGP loan for manufacturing, under the Prime Minister's Employment Generation Programme, is one of the best known. It supports new units with a bank loan combined with a margin money subsidy, which varies by category and location, and applications are made online through the official PMEGP portal run by KVIC, with district industries centres and other agencies involved.

The Pradhan Mantri Mudra Yojana provides loans to small businesses through banks and other lenders in categories for different loan sizes, and it is often used for working capital and smaller machinery. The CGTMSE collateral free loan route is not a separate loan but a credit guarantee that allows banks to lend to micro and small enterprises without demanding property as collateral, which matters most for first-time makers without land to pledge. Stand-Up India supports women and entrepreneurs from SC and ST communities setting up new enterprises, including manufacturing.

[IMAGE 7]

The details of each scheme, including limits, subsidy levels and eligibility, are set by the government and change over time, so I always advise checking the current rules on official portals and with the bank branch. The Ministry of MSME's [official website](https://www.msme.gov.in/) is the right place to start for an overview of schemes, and the bank branch is where the application becomes real.

Sahadev, who is in the middle of his own application, added the most useful practical advice. "The bank does not lend to an idea," he said. "It lends to a project report." A good project report explains the product, the market, the machinery, the costs, the expected sales, the cash flow and the repayment plan, honestly and in detail. I have written about this in [business ideas that need a bank loan](https://ethicalfounder.in/business-ideas-that-need-a-bank-loan/), and every point there applies to manufacturing.

Sudarshini asked whether she, as a woman, had any special route. I told her that several schemes prioritise women entrepreneurs, including Stand-Up India and various state programmes, and that banks are often actively looking for women borrowers with clear plans. "So my paper cone arithmetic will become a project report," she said, and laughed.

## Machinery cost for small factory planning, and how to avoid overspending

The most common mistake I see in new manufacturing units is spending too much on machinery too early. The machinery cost for small factory setups can range widely, from a few lakh rupees for a basic food or craft unit to much more for metalworking or processing equipment, and first-time makers often buy bigger or more automated machines than their first year's sales can justify.

My advice to all five makers was the same. Start with the machine that is the bottleneck, the one without which you cannot produce at all, and do the rest by hand or with simpler tools until sales prove the need. Buy new where reliability is essential and consider well-maintained used machines where they are not. Always ask the seller for references from other small units who bought the same machine.

I have written a detailed example of this thinking in [the payback calculation for a multi-needle embroidery machine](https://ethicalfounder.in/multi-needle-embroidery-machine-brother-pr1060w-payback/), and the same logic works for a pulveriser, a packing machine or a plating setup. Calculate how many units the machine must produce, at what margin, to repay itself, and ask whether your market can absorb that many.

Sudhanshu added a point from experience. "Power cost kills small factories," he said. "Calculate electricity before you calculate profit." He is right, and in recent years energy and fuel costs have moved sharply, as I explained in [how fuel price changes affect small businesses](https://ethicalfounder.in/iran-war-fuel-prices-small-businesses-india/). A unit's real cost per piece must include power, water, transport and packaging, not only raw material and labour.

## Licences, space and the unglamorous work

Beyond Udyam and GST, a small manufacturing unit may need several other permissions depending on its product, size and location. These can include a trade licence from the local body, FSSAI for food products, consent to establish and operate from the State Pollution Control Board for certain categories, a fire safety certificate for some premises, registration under factory and labour laws once workers and power use cross certain thresholds, and specific product standards for some goods.

None of these should frighten a new maker, but all of them should be checked before signing a lease. The single most expensive mistake I have seen is renting a space, buying machines and then discovering that the area does not permit that kind of activity. Before committing, visit the local industries centre or speak to an experienced consultant, and confirm what your product and location require.

Space itself deserves careful thought. A food unit needs clean, washable surfaces and separation from living areas. A plating unit needs ventilation and safe handling of chemicals. A drying unit needs space and airflow. An ancillary parts unit needs power supply and access for transport. Kaka's advice was simple: "Choose the place for the work, not for the rent."

For many small makers, the unglamorous work of records, compliance and paperwork is where help is most valuable. That is where simple digital tools come in, not as the main business but as support, the way I describe in [easy business automation for Indian MSMEs](https://ethicalfounder.in/easy-business-automation-for-indian-msme/) and in [AI tools that help MSMEs with design work](https://ethicalfounder.in/msme-design-automation-ai-tool/). A small maker who keeps clean records from day one will find the bank, the tax authorities and buyers far easier to deal with.

## The money that waits: working capital in a small manufacturing unit

If there is one thing I want every reader to remember, it is this: a small manufacturing unit needs more working capital than most people expect. Raw material must be bought before production, production takes time, finished goods sit in stock, and buyers, especially businesses, often pay weeks or months later. During all that time, wages, rent and power must still be paid.

I walked Subramanya through a simple cash cycle for his jewellery unit. He buys metal and stones today, his artisans make the pieces over two weeks, the pieces sit in his shop or with resellers for some weeks, and resellers pay after they sell. From the day he spends to the day he is paid could be two or three months. If he spends heavily on the first batch, he may not have money for the second before the first is paid for.

This is the reason I keep saying that [the first six months of a small business](https://ethicalfounder.in/money-in-the-first-six-months-of-a-small-business/) decide its future. A unit with a good product can still fail if it runs out of cash while waiting to be paid. Working capital loans, careful credit terms, and starting with a production level the business can afford to wait for are the answers.

For units selling to businesses, MSME rules give some protection against delayed payments from buyers, and Udyam registration is part of using that protection. A simple payment reminder system, like the one I describe in [late payment automation](https://ethicalfounder.in/late-payment-automation/), and a clear daily view of cash, as in [daily business reporting](https://ethicalfounder.in/daily-business-reporting-automation/), help a small maker see trouble before it arrives.

Sudarshini, listening, said her version of this is simpler. "I make today what I can sell this week," she said. "Then I never wait too long." For a food unit with daily sales, that rhythm is exactly right. For a jewellery or parts unit with longer cycles, the principle is the same, only stretched.

## Testing demand before building the unit

Every maker we met had a different way of testing demand before committing fully, and I think this is the wisest part of all their stories. Subramanya will start by making a third of his range and selling it alongside bought pieces, so he learns what customers prefer without risking everything. Dinesh is testing flower-based incense with his existing buyers before building a separate line.

Sudarshini has already been testing for two years without calling it that, selling her snacks on the ghats every evening and learning which ones people come back for. Sahadev has spoken to dozens of farmers and potential buyers before buying a single machine. Sudhanshu built his unit customer by customer over years.

This is the same approach I describe in [testing a business idea before spending](https://ethicalfounder.in/business-ideas-from-a-family-complaint-test-before-spending/) and in [validating a market on zero budget](https://ethicalfounder.in/zero-budget-market-validation-agent/). For a small manufacturing unit, the cheapest test is often to produce a small batch by hand or in a borrowed space, sell it, and only then invest in machines and premises. Research can help too, and I have shown how small businesses can [research markets online efficiently](https://ethicalfounder.in/msme-online-research-automation/) before deciding.

Kaka, who has watched many businesses open and close in Kashi, gave the simplest test of all. "If people in your own lane will not buy it, nobody far away will," he said. It is not always true, but it is a good place to start.

## Selling what you make: local first, then wider

A small maker's first customers are almost always local. Subramanya's resellers and wedding families, Dinesh's temple-road shops, Sudarshini's ghat-side buyers, Sahadev's local food businesses and Sudhanshu's industrial neighbours. This local base is precious, because it gives quick feedback, steady orders and word of mouth.

I reminded all of them that [word of mouth beats advertising for a young business](https://ethicalfounder.in/word-of-mouth-vs-ads-for-a-first-year-business/), and that the best marketing for a small manufacturer is a product that is consistently good. Once the local base is steady, wider markets become possible: distributors in other cities, online marketplaces, institutional buyers and, for some products, exports.

For makers who sell through digital payments, understanding the costs and records of those payments matters, and I have written about [UPI charges for small shops](https://ethicalfounder.in/upi-mdr-charges-small-shops-p2pm-exemption/) and [reading UPI transaction history as a sales report](https://ethicalfounder.in/kenya-fastagger-upi-transaction-history-sales-report-shop-owners/). ## Small manufacturing business ideas India will need next

On our last evening of this part of the journey, Sahadev and I sat with Kaka on the ghat steps and talked about where small manufacturing in India is going. We agreed on a few directions that seem promising for small units in the coming years.

Products that turn waste into value, like Dinesh's temple flowers, agricultural residues into packaging, or used textiles into new goods. Healthy and traditional foods made to modern standards, like Sudarshini's millet snacks and Sahadev's powders. Components and parts for India's growing manufacturing base, like Sudhanshu's fasteners. And craft-based products with strong design and traceable quality, like Subramanya's jewellery.

What unites these manufacturing business ideas India will need is not the product alone. It is the combination of local knowledge, careful numbers, clean compliance and patience. Technology helps, and I am glad to see small units using it, as India's rapid adoption of AI tools shows in [the generative AI adoption survey](https://ethicalfounder.in/india-gen-ai-adoption-score-survey/), but the foundation is always the maker's own discipline.

For readers in smaller towns, many of these ideas work better there than in metros, because land, labour and raw materials are closer and cheaper, a point I explored in [business ideas for small towns](https://ethicalfounder.in/business-ideas-for-small-towns/). ## What the river teaches a maker

When the conversation ended, Subramanya stood up, walked to the edge of the water and stood there for a while, the cremation fires of Harishchandra Ghat burning steadily in the distance. Kaka joined him, and after a moment, so did I. Nobody spoke for a long time.

Then Subramanya said quietly, "My father sold bangles from a basket. I sell from a shop. My son should see us make them." Kaka put a hand on his shoulder and said that every maker in Kashi is, in a small way, doing what Lord Shiva does, creating, sustaining and letting go, and that a man who makes something with honest hands is closer to Mahadev than one who only sells.

In the understanding of karma I grew up with, work done with care is itself a form of worship. A small manufacturing unit is not only a business. It is a place where raw material becomes something useful, where people earn wages to feed their families, and where an owner's character shows in every piece that leaves the door. That is why the mistakes matter, and why the careful planning matters even more.

I have also learnt, on this journey, that the best makers do not try to do everything themselves. They know when to bring in help, as I wrote in [what happens when a small business owner stops doing everything](https://ethicalfounder.in/when-a-small-business-owner-stops-doing-everything/), and they know their margins as well as [a careful small-town pharmacist knows his](https://ethicalfounder.in/small-town-pharmacy-business-lessons-retail-margins/).

## Questions People Ask About Starting a Small Manufacturing Unit

**How do I start a small manufacturing unit in India?**

Choose a product with proven local demand, test it with a small batch, prepare a project report with honest costs and cash flow, register on Udyam, obtain GST and product-specific licences, arrange space and machinery, and apply for finance through a bank or government scheme.

**Is Udyam registration for manufacturers free?**

Yes, Udyam registration is free on the official government portal and uses Aadhaar and PAN, so avoid websites that charge fees for the same process.

**What is a PMEGP loan for manufacturing?**

PMEGP is a government programme that supports new units with a bank loan combined with a margin money subsidy, which varies by category and location, and applications are made online through the official PMEGP portal.

**What is a CGTMSE collateral free loan?**

CGTMSE is a credit guarantee scheme that allows banks and lenders to give loans to micro and small enterprises without asking for property as collateral, with the guarantee covering part of the lender's risk.

**What are good manufacturing business ideas in India for small units?**

Promising ideas include craft products like imitation jewellery, waste-to-value products like temple flower incense, healthy foods like millet snacks, vegetable powder processing, and ancillary parts for local industries.

**How much does machinery cost for a small factory?**

Machinery cost for a small factory varies widely by product and scale, from a few lakh rupees for basic food or craft setups to much more for metalworking, so start with the bottleneck machine and calculate its payback first.

**Do I need FSSAI for millet snack manufacturing?**

Yes, any food manufacturing business needs FSSAI registration or a licence depending on scale, along with proper hygiene and labelling.

**Can I start imitation jewellery manufacturing from home?**

A very small start may be possible, but processes like plating need ventilation, safe chemical handling and possibly pollution consent, so a separate, suitable space is usually wiser once production grows.

**What is a floral waste recycling business?**

It is a business that collects flowers offered at temples or used at events and turns them into products such as incense, natural dyes, handmade paper or compost.

**How much working capital does a small manufacturing unit need?**

Enough to cover raw material, wages, rent and power from the day you spend until the day buyers pay, which can be several months, so plan production to a level your cash can sustain.
