# Loan processing fee fraud took two thousand rupees each from women who wanted to grow, and a snack seller on the ghats told me why it works

## Seventy-five chairs that were never there

Shankar Kaka does not organise events. He organises evenings. There is a difference, and I understood it only when I saw what he had arranged for us on the steps a little upstream of Harishchandra Ghat. No chairs, no banner, no microphone.

[IMAGE 1]

Just a wide stone landing, a few borrowed mats, two big kettles of tea from Munna's stall, a basket of hot pakoras that nobody seemed to have paid for, and, by the time the lamps were lit, more than seventy-five small business owners sitting in loose circles, talking over each other.

There were tea sellers and flower sellers, a pani puri man who kept getting up to serve, two women who stitch blouses at home, a young couple who run a mobile recharge counter, an elderly widow who sells puja material from a cloth on the ground, a kirana owner from the lane behind the ghat, a boatman or two, and several aunties who had come only because their neighbours were coming.

Kaka had told them that a writer from Hyderabad who helps small businesses with automation wanted to listen. That was enough.

In Varanasi, if people feel you have come with respect, they give you their evening and ask for nothing in return.

We laughed a great deal, we ate a great deal, and somewhere between the second round of tea and the pani puri, the conversation turned to money, and then to loans, and then to a story from Bihar that half the circle had already heard.

I am writing this the following morning, still a little hoarse from talking, with the river very calm and the notebook full.

## Sudarshini aunty, and the question that stopped me

Before I tell the Bihar story, I have to introduce the person who changed the evening. Her name is Sudarshini, and everyone on the ghats calls her aunty.

She sells homemade fried snacks, the crisp, salty kind people eat while sitting on the steps at dusk, and she sells them herself, walking from group to group with a steel container and a stack of paper cones, ten rupees, twenty rupees, sometimes a little more for a family. She had been sitting slightly apart, watching, her container on her knees.

I made the mistake of asking her, half joking, why she was sitting there observing like a manager instead of selling. She looked at me for a moment and then said, in Hindi, something I have not stopped thinking about.

"क्या हम जिंदगी ऐसे ही निकालते रहेंगे?" Are we supposed to spend our whole life like this, she asked.

"Don't you think I will grow? That my husband will one day leave the job that eats all his days for a small company, and we will start a business together, he and I? Why can't you imagine that? We can see a future for our daughter too. Can't you see it?" I laughed, then apologised, and Kaka made space for her in the circle.

I asked her, gently, how much she earns in a day. Between one thousand and fifteen hundred rupees, she said, depending on the crowd. Her husband earns a small fixed salary at a company near the city. Their daughter studies in a government school and is, by her mother's account, the cleverest girl in her class.

They do not buy phones on monthly instalments, they do not buy anything shiny, they save, and slowly, piece by piece, they have been putting money into a small plot of land outside the city. "Small is only a word," she said.

"Big businessmen also started with one basket." The whole circle went quiet when she said it. I have met founders with large offices who do not think as clearly about growth as Sudarshini aunty does with a steel container on her knees.

And it was Sudarshini who first said the words that took the evening to Bihar. "People like us need loans to grow," she said. "And people like us are exactly who the loan agents come for."

## What happened in Rosera: the Samastipur loan fraud

The kirana owner from the lane behind the ghat had relatives in Samastipur district, and he told the story the way people tell news from home, with more feeling than detail. I read the detail afterwards in [Prabhat Khabar's Samastipur report](https://www.prabhatkhabar.com/state/bihar/samastipur/samastipur-rosera-loan-fraud-150-women).

[IMAGE 2]

According to that report, in the Rosera police station area, women were promised loans of two lakh rupees each by a man who showed an identity card and introduced himself as a branch manager of a company.

He assured them that their loans would be approved quickly. For this, he collected two thousand rupees from each person as a processing fee and file charge, from around 150 people. When the women went to the office of the company they had been told about, they found no staff there.

Angry, they raised an alarm and informed the police, and a complaint was given at the police station asking for a case to be registered and action to be taken.

The report carried a photograph of the women standing outside the police station holding up their bank passbooks. That image is what stayed with the kirana owner.

"They showed their passbooks," he said, "as if to say, look, we have accounts, we are real people, we only wanted a loan." This samastipur loan fraud is small by the standards of the crore-rupee cases that make national headlines.

Two thousand rupees each. But for a woman who sells vegetables or stitches blouses, two thousand rupees can be a week of profit or the deposit on a sewing machine. And the harm is not only the money. It is the next time a genuine loan officer visits the village, and nobody trusts him.

## Why loan processing fee fraud works on careful people

The widow who sells puja material said something sharp. "They did not take two lakh," she said. "They took two thousand. Two thousand is small enough that you think, if it is a lie, I lose a little. If it is true, I get two lakh." That is exactly the design of loan processing fee fraud.

The amount asked is chosen to feel like a reasonable risk. It is also small enough that many victims never go to the police, because the trip, the time and the shame cost more than the loss.

The agent collects from a hundred and fifty people and disappears before anyone compares notes. Kaka added the second piece. "He came with an ID card," he said.

"A card makes a stranger into an officer." A fake bank manager id is among the cheapest tools a fraudster can have.

A printed card with a photograph, a logo-like design and a designation such as branch manager or field officer costs very little to make, and today it can be designed in minutes with image tools, which is one reason I keep encouraging people to learn [how to check whether an image or document is AI generated](https://ethicalfounder.in/check-if-image-is-ai-generated-synthid-detector/).

The third piece came from Sudarshini herself. "Women are told that loans are hard for us," she said. "So when a man comes to our door and says it will be easy, we want to believe him." Business loan for women sellers is a real and growing area of banking in India, with government-backed schemes and banks actively looking for women borrowers.

But the fact that real help exists is precisely what makes the fake offer believable. The fraudster borrows the language of real schemes.

## The loan agent fraud playbook and the file charge for loan approval

By now, several people in the circle had their own versions. A flower seller said a man had come to her basti offering a "Mudra loan" through his "contact in the bank" for a fee of five hundred rupees per form.

A young recharge-shop owner said his cousin in a small town near Prayagraj had paid a "file charge for loan" twice, once for the application and once for "verification".

The pani puri man laughed and said nobody had offered him anything because he looks too poor, and everyone laughed with him.

Putting their stories together, the loan agent fraud playbook looks like this. The agent arrives in person, usually in a group of women or small sellers, often through a local woman who has been promised a commission.

He speaks with confidence about a well-known scheme, a bank or a finance company, and shows an identity card.

He promises a large loan amount, quick approval and no collateral. He asks for a small advance fee before loan approval, called a processing fee, a file charge, a form fee, an insurance fee or a stamp charge.

He collects cash or a UPI transfer to a personal number. He gives a date for disbursement, sometimes a printed receipt. He disappears, or keeps asking for "one more charge" until people stop paying.

A mudra loan agent who asks for money to get you a loan is almost always this playbook, because the real scheme does not need middlemen collecting fees from borrowers at their doorstep.

## What a real loan never asks you for, starting with an advance fee before loan approval

I promised the circle a short list they could remember without writing anything down, because many of them do not read comfortably. Kaka repeated each point in Bhojpuri after me, and the aunties repeated it after him.

[IMAGE 4]

1. A real bank or regulated lender never sends a stranger to collect a fee in cash or to a personal UPI number at your door.
2. A real loan application is made at the bank branch, through the bank's own official app or website, or through a government portal or Common Service Centre, and any charge is shown in writing in the loan terms, never demanded before you even apply.
3. A real loan officer can be verified by calling the branch on its official number, which you find yourself, not the number on the card he gives you.
4. A real loan amount is decided after your documents and repayment capacity are checked, never promised as a round figure to everyone in a group.
5. A real loan is paid only into your own bank account in your name, never handed over by an agent.
6. Any advance fee before loan approval, paid to a person rather than shown in a bank's written terms, is your signal to stop.
The widow repeated number six twice and then said, "So if he asks first, he is a thief." It is a rough rule, but for doorstep agents it is close to the truth.

## Where the real help is: the Mudra loan agent you do not need and the PM SVANidhi loan

The young couple from the recharge counter asked the most practical question of the evening. "If not the agent, then where?" I explained the honest routes as simply as I could, because they exist and they work.

The Pradhan Mantri Mudra Yojana supports small non-farm businesses with loans through banks and other lenders, and the scheme's official information is on the [MUDRA website](https://www.mudra.org.in/).

For street vendors in particular, the PM SVANidhi loan scheme was created to give small working-capital loans to vendors through banks, applied for through official channels, with the vendor's own documents.

A small loan for street vendors of this kind is designed for exactly people like Sudarshini aunty, the flower sellers and the pani puri man. The bank branch nearest to your home or shop is still the most reliable door, and many branches have staff who handle these schemes.

Banks may charge a processing fee on some loans as part of their written terms, but that is disclosed, recorded and taken through the bank, never collected by a stranger in a basti.

I also told them what I tell every first-time borrower: a loan is a tool, not a gift, and it should be taken for something that earns back more than it costs. I have written about this in detail in [business ideas that need a bank loan](https://ethicalfounder.in/business-ideas-that-need-a-bank-loan/), and the same logic applies whether the loan is two lakh or twenty thousand.

## Sudarshini's arithmetic and the business loan for women sellers

Later, when the circle had broken into smaller groups and the pani puri man was finally sitting down, I asked Sudarshini aunty to tell me more about her plan. She took a paper cone, opened it flat and did the arithmetic in pencil while I watched.

[IMAGE 5]

Her snacks bring between one thousand and fifteen hundred a day. Her husband's salary is fixed. If together they could add even twenty thousand more each month through a small family business of their own, their household would reach about one lakh a month. "Twelve lakh a year," she said, tapping the paper. "From snacks and a small shop. Who says we are small?"

Her plan is a little food business with a proper counter near a school or a temple road, her recipes, her husband handling purchase and accounts, and their daughter, when she is older, managing orders on the phone.

She does not want a loan to start it. She wants a loan to grow it, after she has proven that people will come. That order of steps is wiser than most business plans I see from people with degrees.

I told her about [testing an idea before spending](https://ethicalfounder.in/business-ideas-from-a-family-complaint-test-before-spending/), and she said she had been doing exactly that for two years without knowing it had a name.

I also told her that a business like hers, run by a husband and wife, needs to keep its money separate from household money from the very first day, which I explain in [money in the first six months of a small business](https://ethicalfounder.in/money-in-the-first-six-months-of-a-small-business/).

She nodded and said, "This is why I am observing. I want to know how the big people do it." I think that sentence describes the whole purpose of this journey better than I could.

## How a little automation protects people like her

Kaka and I spent the next morning with Sudarshini aunty and two of the flower sellers, and we did something very small that I want every reader to consider.

We set up a simple daily sales note on her phone, three lines a day, money in, money spent and what sold best, so that when she goes to a bank branch she can show a steady record instead of only her word.

Banks look kindly on records, and records also protect against agents, because a person who knows her own numbers is harder to fool with promises of "easy money".

For the flower sellers, who take UPI payments all day, I showed them how their payment history already works as a record, and how the small charges that may appear on [UPI payments for small shops](https://ethicalfounder.in/upi-mdr-charges-small-shops-p2pm-exemption/) should be understood before they worry about them.

We also saved the official numbers of the two nearest bank branches in each of their phones, so that the next time a man with a card arrives, they can call the branch while he is still standing there.

This is the kind of automation I believe in: nothing grand, nothing that needs a subscription, only a few habits that make a small seller stronger. I have collected more of these in [easy business automation for Indian MSMEs](https://ethicalfounder.in/easy-business-automation-for-indian-msme/).

Sudarshini aunty said she would teach her daughter to keep the record, "so that she learns business before she learns fear."

## What the mobile recharge couple taught me about fake bank manager ID cards

On the morning after the gathering, the young couple from the recharge counter came to find us on the steps, carrying a plastic folder.

[IMAGE 3]

Inside were three identity cards that agents had shown in their town over the past year, cards that the agents had carelessly left behind or that people had photographed and printed out.

Each claimed a different bank or finance company, each had a confident designation, and none of them, when we called the branch numbers we found ourselves, belonged to anyone who worked there. The husband said the cards had become better looking every season.

A fake bank manager id once looked like a wedding invitation, he said, and now it looks like it came from a head office. That matches what I have seen elsewhere: the tools that help honest small businesses design visiting cards and price lists also help fraudsters design credentials, and a laminated card proves only that someone owns a printer.

We agreed on a simple habit for the couple's shop, which sees dozens of customers a day. They pasted the official numbers of three nearby bank branches on the counter wall, and whenever a customer mentions a loan agent, they call the branch together, on speaker, while the customer listens. Within a week, they told Kaka, two customers had decided not to pay an agent.

Word of mouth, which I keep saying [beats any advertisement for a small business](https://ethicalfounder.in/word-of-mouth-vs-ads-for-a-first-year-business/), works just as well as a warning system when one trusted counter starts it. The couple also asked about WhatsApp messages from "loan desks" that reply instantly and politely.

I explained what a genuine business is allowed to do with automated chats, which I covered in [the rules on WhatsApp AI chatbots](https://ethicalfounder.in/whatsapp-ai-chatbot-rules-2026/), and why a bot that asks for a file charge for loan processing to a personal UPI number is never a bank's bot.

For their own shop, we set up a short reminder message for customers who owe small amounts, the honest kind of automation I describe in [late payment automation](https://ethicalfounder.in/late-payment-automation/), and a one-line daily sales note like the one in [daily business reporting](https://ethicalfounder.in/daily-business-reporting-automation/), so that their own records are ready the day they walk into a bank.

## If you have already paid in a loan processing fee fraud

Several people in the circle knew someone who had already paid. For them, and for anyone reading this after paying an agent, these are the steps.

Write down everything you remember: the agent's name, the name of the company or bank he claimed, his phone number, the date and place he came, the amount you paid and how. Gather receipts, UPI transaction IDs and any photo you took of his identity card.

Speak with others in your area who paid, because a group complaint carries more weight than one person's two thousand rupees.

Go to the local police station together and give a written complaint, as the women in Rosera did, and ask for a receipt or a registered case number. If the agent claimed to represent a specific bank, inform that bank's branch in writing, because banks take misuse of their name seriously.

Report the phone number and UPI ID used to collect the fee through the official channels for financial fraud reporting.

Do not pay any further "charge" to recover the first one, which is often the second stage of the same fraud.

And do not let shame keep you silent, because silence is what allows the same man to visit the next village.

When the gathering ended, well past ten, the steps were scattered with paper cones and tea cups, and a few of the aunties stayed to sweep them up without being asked. Kaka and I walked down to the water's edge, where the cremation fires of Harishchandra Ghat were still burning steadily, and sat for a while without speaking.

Kaka said that people come to Kashi believing it is a city of endings, but that the boatmen know it is a city of beginnings, because every person who leaves the burning ghat leaves with a clearer idea of what matters. I thought of Sudarshini's question: are we supposed to spend our whole life like this?

In the understanding of karma I grew up with, the answer is no, because action itself is our right, and the honest effort to grow is one of the purest actions a person can take. The agent who took two thousand rupees from each woman in Rosera acted against that dharma.

He took from people at the exact moment they were trying to rise, and in my belief that is among the heaviest karma a person can carry.

But the women who went to the police with their passbooks held high, and the snack seller who is saving for a plot of land while doing her arithmetic on a paper cone, are acting on the other side of the same law.

Lord Shiva is worshipped in this city as the one who destroys so that something new can begin.

## Questions People Ask About Loan Processing Fee Fraud

**What is loan processing fee fraud?**

It is a fraud in which an agent promises a loan and collects an advance processing fee, file charge or form fee in cash or to a personal account, then disappears without any loan being given.

**What happened in the Samastipur loan fraud?**

According to a Prabhat Khabar report, a man claiming to be a branch manager promised women in the Rosera area two lakh rupee loans and collected two thousand rupees each from around 150 people as processing fee and file charge, and the stated office was found empty.

**Do banks charge a file charge for loan applications?**

Some banks charge a processing fee on certain loans as part of their written terms, but it is disclosed and handled by the bank, never collected by a stranger at your door before you apply.

**How do I recognise loan agent fraud?**

Watch for a stranger offering large quick loans to a whole group, showing an identity card, asking for an advance fee in cash or to a personal UPI number, and avoiding the bank branch.

**Is a Mudra loan agent who charges a fee genuine?**

Mudra loans are given through banks and other lenders, and you can apply directly, so any agent who demands money to get you a Mudra loan should be treated with great caution.

**How can I check a fake bank manager ID?**

Call the bank branch on its official number that you find yourself, ask whether the person works there, and never rely on the phone number printed on the card he shows.

**What business loan options exist for women sellers?**

Women sellers can approach bank branches for schemes such as Mudra loans and other government-backed programmes, and apply only through the bank, official portals or Common Service Centres.

**What is the PM SVANidhi loan?**

PM SVANidhi is a government scheme that provides small working-capital loans to street vendors through banks, applied for through official channels with the vendor's own documents.

**Is any advance fee before loan approval legal?**

A fee shown in a bank's written loan terms can be legitimate, but an advance fee demanded by an individual before any application or approval is a classic warning sign of fraud.

**What should I do if I paid a processing fee to a fake agent?**

Write down every detail, collect receipts and transaction IDs, complain to the police with others who paid, inform the bank whose name was used, report the number and UPI ID, and pay nothing more.
