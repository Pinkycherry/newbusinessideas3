# A share trading fraud handed out notarised agreements and signed cheques, and a classroom of Patna business owners asked me why the paper failed

## Priyanka Rathod's classroom on the third floor

Priyanka Rathod runs her business coaching classes from a third-floor office in Patna, above a sweet shop whose smell climbs the stairs and follows you into the room.

[IMAGE 1]

She is a business coach who still believes in offline classes, with chairs, a whiteboard, printed handouts and a tea break where the real questions get asked.

Shankar Kaka had met her years ago through a mutual friend, and when she heard we were travelling through, she insisted we come.

The room that afternoon held about thirty people: a hardware dealer, two women who run a tiffin service, a young man with a mobile accessories shop, a retired schoolteacher who has opened a coaching centre, a cloth merchant, a pharmacist, a printing press owner and several others whose businesses I learnt one by one over tea.

Priyanka introduced me as someone who writes about how small business owners are cheated, and asked me to talk about whatever was on my mind. I had planned to talk about automation.

Within five minutes, the hardware dealer raised his hand and asked about a case he had read about from Chhattisgarh, where investors had been given notarised agreements and bank cheques and had still lost everything.

"We are always told to get papers," he said. "These people had papers. So what is the use?"

It was the right question, and the whole afternoon became an answer to it. I am writing this a day later, on the road, with Priyanka's handouts folded inside my notebook.

## What the Dhamtari share trading case shows about the Wealth Tracker Dhamtari office

The case he meant was reported by [Amar Ujala from Dhamtari](https://www.amarujala.com/chhattisgarh/accused-of-duping-rs-3-12-crore-in-the-name-of-share-trading-arrested-from-indore-2026-11-01). According to that report, Dhamtari police arrested the main accused, Vikram Singh Shah, from Indore in Madhya Pradesh in a share trading and investment case involving more than ₹3.12 crore.

The report says he ran an office called Wealth Tracker on Collectorate Road in Dhamtari, from where he encouraged people to put money into the share market and trading apps. The method, as described in the report, was to promise investors four percent profit or interest every month. To win their trust, he prepared notarised agreements on stamp paper and gave investors bank cheques as security.

A sixty-two-year-old resident of Gurur in Balod district filed the complaint, and the report says he and his family had invested ₹25 lakh. For the first few months, profits were paid, which kept the trust alive, and then the accused allegedly disappeared without returning either the profit or the principal.

As with every case I write about, these are allegations at the stage of investigation, and the accused will have his own defence in court.

But the shape of the wealth tracker dhamtari office matters for every small business owner, because it used the very tools we usually tell people to insist on: a real office, a written agreement, a notary's stamp and a signed cheque.

That is why the hardware dealer's question was so sharp. The retired schoolteacher in the front row put it in one line. "So he gave them everything we teach our children to ask for," she said, "except the truth."

## Lesson one: a notarised agreement investment is a record, not a guarantee

I wrote the first lesson on Priyanka's whiteboard, and the room copied it down. A notarised agreement investment proves that two people signed a paper in front of a notary on a certain day. It does not prove that the business described in the paper exists, that the promised return is possible, or that the person signing has the money or the intention to pay.

[IMAGE 2]

A notary verifies identity and signature. A notary does not audit a trading strategy. Many investors, especially in smaller towns, treat the notary's stamp as if it were a government approval of the deal, and the fraudster knows this. The stamp turns a promise into something that looks official. The agreement is still useful, and I told the room clearly not to stop asking for papers.

In a court case, a written agreement helps prove what was promised, how much was paid and when. But a paper protects you after something goes wrong. It does not stop the thing from going wrong.

The only thing that does that is checking, before you sign, whether the promise could ever be true.

## Lesson two: a security cheque investment can bounce like any other cheque

The tiffin-service sisters asked about the cheques, which surprised many people in the room. "If he gave a cheque," the elder sister asked, "can't they just deposit it?" They can, and many will. But a security cheque investment is only as strong as the account it is drawn on. If the account has no money, the cheque will bounce.

[IMAGE 3]

A cheque bounce after investment does give the holder a legal route under the Negotiable Instruments Act, with a written notice and then a complaint if the amount is not paid, and that route can be powerful.

It is also slow, it needs the drawer to be found, and when dozens or hundreds of cheques bounce at once from the same person, the money behind them is usually gone. Priyanka added something from her own experience.

She said that in many towns, giving a cheque has become a performance of trust, a way of saying "see, I am serious," and that people now ask for cheques almost by habit without asking where the money will come from to honour them. "A cheque is a promise written on paper," she said. "It is still a promise."

I told the room that a cheque bounce after investment should be acted on quickly, to keep any security cheque safe, to note its date carefully because cheques have a validity period, and to act on it promptly if repayment stops. But I also told them never to treat a cheque as a reason to invest.

## Lesson three: monthly interest on investment from the share market is a contradiction

The printing press owner, who said he had dabbled in shares himself, made the third point before I could. "The share market goes up and down," he said. "How can anyone promise a fixed monthly interest on investment from shares?" That is the heart of every share trading fraud I have looked at.

The share market can give good returns over time, and it can also fall sharply in a month or a year. Any genuine product linked to shares carries that uncertainty.

A promise of fixed monthly interest on money invested in shares is either a lie about the investment or a sign that the "profit" is being paid from new investors' money, which is the old pattern of every collapsing scheme.

I drew a simple comparison on the board to show the difference between what such offices say and what regulated products actually do. The room photographed it, and Priyanka said she would add it to her handout.

| What the share market investment office offers | What a regulated route actually looks like |
|---|---|
| Fixed monthly profit or interest on your money | No fixed return; value moves with the market |
| Your money paid to the office or the owner's account | Your money stays in your own demat and trading account with a registered broker |
| A notarised agreement and a security cheque | Contract notes from the exchange for every trade, and statements in your name |
| The owner "trades for you" with your funds | You place trades yourself, or a registered portfolio manager acts under a regulated agreement |
| Advice from the office owner | Advice only from a SEBI-registered investment adviser or research analyst, whose registration you can check |

## Lesson four: check for a SEBI registered investment adviser before you trust advice

The young man from the mobile accessories shop asked how he could tell whether the person advising him on shares was genuine. That question has a clear answer, and I wish more people knew it.

[IMAGE 4]

In India, anyone who gives investment advice for a fee must be registered with SEBI as an investment adviser, and anyone who gives research recommendations on shares must be registered as a research analyst. Brokers, portfolio managers and other intermediaries are also registered.

You can search their names and registration numbers on SEBI's public records, and SEBI also runs an [investor education website](https://investor.sebi.gov.in/) that explains in simple language how to check intermediaries and how to complain.

A sebi registered investment adviser will give you a registration number, a written agreement under SEBI's rules, and will not take your money into their own account to trade on your behalf.

A share market investment office that asks you to hand over your funds so that the owner can trade them and pay you a fixed monthly amount is not operating as a registered adviser, whatever its signboard says. The young man said that the person who had approached him had a beautiful office with three computers and a big screen showing charts.

"That is the decoration," the hardware dealer said from the back. "The registration is the building." The whole room laughed, and the young man wrote it down.

## Shanti Kulkarni's question about trust

Sitting near the window that afternoon was Shanti Kulkarni, who holds a PhD from Jawaharlal Nehru University in New Delhi and has worked for years on financial literacy among small entrepreneurs.

[IMAGE 5]

Priyanka had invited her to observe the class, and during the tea break she asked me a question that went deeper than any lesson on the board.

"Why do intelligent people keep trusting the form of safety instead of the substance?" she asked. She explained that in her research, people in smaller towns often rely on familiar rituals of trust, a stamp, a signature, a cheque, an office, an introduction from a known person, because those rituals have worked for generations in local trade. The fraudster simply learns the rituals.

I thought she was exactly right, and I said so. The answer, I suggested, is to add one new ritual to the old ones: the ritual of checking a registration. Just as people now habitually ask for a cheque, they need to habitually ask for a SEBI registration number and look it up.

Shanti said she would write that into the training material she is preparing for women entrepreneurs, and Priyanka said she would add a ten-minute "registration check" exercise to every class she teaches. Shanti also pointed out something about the pattern of victims that I had not considered.

She said that retired people and families, rather than active traders, are often the first to trust such offices, because they want a steady monthly income to replace a salary or pension. That matches the Dhamtari report, where a retired man and his family invested together. The promise of monthly interest is aimed precisely at people who need monthly money.

## Lesson five: why a share market investment office pays on time at first

The cloth merchant asked the question that every victim asks afterwards. "If it was a fraud, why did it pay for months?" The answer is the oldest trick in the book, and it is also the reason these schemes spread. A share market investment office that pays its first investors on time is not proving its trading skill.

It is buying their trust and their word of mouth with money that, in many such cases, comes from the next investors. In the Dhamtari report, profits were paid for the first few months, which kept the complainant's family confident.

I have seen the same rhythm in the [fake trading apps that show profits you cannot withdraw](https://ethicalfounder.in/fake-trading-app-profits-cant-withdraw/), in chit-style schemes and in daily-income apps. Early payments are the scheme's marketing budget. The longer they continue, the more people join, and the bigger the collapse when new money slows down.

So I gave the room a simple rule: judge an investment by how it earns, not by how it pays.

If you cannot see the trades, the contract notes and the account in your own name, the monthly payment tells you nothing about whether your capital is safe. Priyanka added that this is the same rule she teaches about customers, that a customer who pays the first two orders on time and then asks for a large credit is the one to watch.

The pharmacist laughed and said he had a customer exactly like that. Everyone had one. And that, I said, is the point: business owners already know this pattern from their own counters. They only forget it when the person asking for trust is offering them money instead of asking for credit.

If you are tempted by a steady monthly income from outside your business, my notes on [the difference between a side income and a registered business](https://ethicalfounder.in/side-income-vs-registered-business/) explain why the steadiest income is usually the one you can see and control.

## Investors cheated in Chhattisgarh, and the Indore link

The cloth merchant had relatives in Raipur, and he said the case had shaken many families there. Investors cheated in Chhattisgarh by such offices are not rare, he said, and many of the accused seem to move between states, which is why the arrest in Indore mattered.

An office that runs in one district and an owner who is found in another is a pattern that makes recovery harder and complaints more scattered.

I pointed out that this is one more reason to check registrations before investing, because a registered intermediary has a recorded address and a regulator that can act across states. An unregistered office has only its signboard, and a signboard does not follow its owner to another city.

Kaka, who had been quiet in the corner, said that in Kashi the boatmen have a saying: a boat that carries everyone for free in the morning will ask for the whole fare at night. The room laughed, and the cloth merchant said he would use that line with his own relatives. Then the pharmacist added a practical point.

He said that in his town, people are embarrassed to complain about small amounts, but the Dhamtari case shows how dozens of small and medium investments add up to crores.

"If one person had complained early," he said, "maybe the others would not have joined." Priyanka asked everyone to promise that if they ever saw such an office in Patna, they would tell her, and she would bring the information to her class and to the police.

That is how a community protects itself, and it is the same principle I describe in [why word of mouth beats any advertisement](https://ethicalfounder.in/word-of-mouth-vs-ads-for-a-first-year-business/): the trust that spreads a scheme can also spread the warning against it.

## What to do if your money is in a share trading fraud

Before the class ended, Priyanka asked me to give the room a short list they could follow if they, or someone they knew, were already inside such a scheme.

I repeated the steps I give every reader, adjusted to this kind of case. Stop all further payments immediately, including any "renewal" or "upgrade" fee. Collect every document: the notarised agreement, the security cheque, receipts, bank transfer records, messages and the address of the office.

If the cheque is still within its validity period and payments have stopped, consult a lawyer quickly about presenting it and the notice process under the cheque bounce law.

Join the complaint that the police are already handling if a case exists, rather than filing alone in a different station, and ask for a receipt or case number.

If the person claimed to be a SEBI-registered entity, report that claim to SEBI through its official complaint channel. Keep your own business accounts separate from this loss, so that your shop or service is not dragged down with it.

And speak to your family openly, because hiding the loss usually makes the next decision worse. For business owners, I added one more point.

Rebuild your cash buffer before taking any new risk, the way I describe in [money in the first six months of a small business](https://ethicalfounder.in/money-in-the-first-six-months-of-a-small-business/), and if your shop needs capital to recover, go to a bank with a clear plan, as I explain in [business ideas that need a bank loan](https://ethicalfounder.in/business-ideas-that-need-a-bank-loan/).

## Automation in Priyanka's office, and what the class asked for

After the formal session, the conversation turned to the topic I had originally planned, and the room was full of practical questions. The tiffin-service sisters wanted a way to take daily orders without answering forty phone calls every morning. The printing press owner wanted automatic reminders for customers who take delivery and pay late.

The pharmacist wanted a simple daily report of what sold and what is running out. We spent an hour on these, and I was struck by how closely they connected to the fraud lesson. People who can see their own numbers clearly, every day, are far less tempted by a stranger's promise of fixed monthly income.

A shop owner who knows that his business earns a certain amount in a good month and less in a bad one will laugh at anyone who promises a fixed share-market return.

Priyanka asked whether Kaka and I would come back to run a full automation session for her students. Kaka answered before I could, as usual, and said yes.

## The road to Gaya and the river that asks for nothing

The next morning, Priyanka travelled with us to Gaya. She had family there and wanted to show us the old city by the Falgu river, and the three-hour drive in Kaka's four-wheeler became an extended continuation of the class.

[IMAGE 6]

She asked about AI tools for her coaching business, about how to use simple automation to follow up with students, and about how to explain all of this to people who still prefer handwritten notes.

Shanti Kulkarni joined us for part of the way, and the two women argued happily about whether people learn more from fear or from hope. Shanti said fear makes people check, and hope makes people grow. Priyanka said a good teacher uses both.

I said that the karma of a teacher is to give both, in the right measure, and Kaka said that is why Shiva is both the destroyer and the teacher of the world.

In Gaya, we stood by the Falgu, which at that time was mostly a wide bed of sand with thin streams of water, and Priyanka explained that the river is said to flow beneath the surface even when it looks dry. Kaka said that is how honest money works too. It does not show off on the surface. It keeps flowing quietly underneath, season after season, the way a well-run shop's earnings do.

On the way back to the car, Priyanka stopped at a small tea stall by the ghat and bought tea for everyone, and the stall owner, an old man with a white beard, refused to take money from Kaka because, he said, guides who bring people to Gaya with respect are his guests. Priyanka laughed and said this is the kind of trust no notary can stamp.

Shanti said it is the kind of trust her research cannot measure. I said it is the kind of trust every honest business is trying to earn, one customer at a time.

I thought of the Dhamtari investors, who were shown a river of monthly profit on the surface with nothing flowing beneath it.

In my understanding of karma, every promise we make carries its own fruit, and a promise made to people who needed monthly income for their families is a heavy promise to break. The ones who broke it will meet that fruit in their own time.

The rest of us carry a lighter duty: to check, to ask for the registration, and to teach the next person to do the same. A share trading fraud can print an agreement, stamp it, sign a cheque and decorate an office with screens. It cannot print a SEBI registration that matches the public record.

For any small business owner reading this, that one check is the difference between a paper that looks safe and an investment that is.

## Questions People Ask About Share Trading Fraud

**What is a share trading fraud?**

It is a scheme in which someone collects money promising to trade shares on your behalf and pay a fixed monthly profit, then pays early returns from new investors' money and eventually stops paying and disappears.

**What happened in the Dhamtari share trading case?**

According to an Amar Ujala report, police arrested the main accused from Indore for allegedly collecting more than ₹3.12 crore through an office called Wealth Tracker in Dhamtari, promising monthly profit and giving notarised agreements and bank cheques as security.

**Does a notarised agreement investment protect my money?**

A notarised agreement records what was promised and who signed, and it helps in court, but it does not prove the business is real or that the promised return is possible, so it is not protection on its own.

**What can I do if a security cheque bounces after investment?**

You can send a legal notice under the cheque bounce provisions and file a complaint if the amount is not paid within the notice period, so consult a lawyer quickly and keep the cheque's validity date in mind.

**Can anyone promise fixed monthly interest on investment in shares?**

No genuine share market product can promise a fixed monthly return, because share prices rise and fall, so any such promise is a strong warning sign.

**How do I check a SEBI registered investment adviser?**

Ask for the adviser's SEBI registration number and full name, search it on SEBI's public records of registered intermediaries, and confirm that your money stays in your own account.

**Is a share market investment office with screens and staff safe?**

An office, screens and staff prove nothing about registration; what matters is whether the entity is registered with SEBI for what it is doing and whether your money stays in your own name.

**Why are investors cheated in Chhattisgarh and other states by such offices?**

Such offices often target retired people and families who want steady monthly income, use familiar trust signals like stamps and cheques, and the accused may move to other states, which scatters complaints.

**What should I do if my money is stuck in a share trading fraud?**

Stop all payments, collect your agreement, cheque and transfer records, act quickly on any security cheque with legal advice, join the existing police complaint, and report false SEBI registration claims to SEBI.

**What was the Wealth Tracker Dhamtari office?**

According to the Amar Ujala report, Wealth Tracker was the name of the office on Collectorate Road in Dhamtari from which the accused allegedly encouraged people to invest in shares and trading apps for monthly profit.
