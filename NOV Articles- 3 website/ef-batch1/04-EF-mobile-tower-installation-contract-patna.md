# How a mobile tower installation contract and a fifteen-year acquaintance became a costly lesson for a Patna businessman

## Fragrance on the edge of the city

The morning after the boat, Shankar Kaka woke me at seven with a cup of tea and an announcement. "Today you will see a real factory," he said, in the tone he uses when he has already decided something on my behalf.

[IMAGE 1]

We drove out in his old four-wheeler past the crowded lanes, past the bridge where the river opens wide, and into a quieter stretch on the edge of the city where small workshops sit between fields and new houses.

Kaka stopped in front of a blue metal gate with no signboard and a smell that reached us before the gate opened. Sandalwood, camphor, and something sweeter that I could not name.

This is the agarbatti and dhoop workshop of Dinesh Gupta, a man Kaka has known since his first year in Kashi, and one of the people we came here to meet.

Dinesh makes incense sticks and dhoop cones that are sold in small shops near the temples and ghats, and he employs about a dozen women from the nearby villages who roll, dip and dry the sticks by hand. Part of the reason for our visit was practical.

Dinesh takes orders from shops by phone, keeps stock in a notebook, and loses track of who has paid and who has not, especially during the busy pilgrim months.

Kaka had told him that I might be able to help him set up something simple, and Dinesh had been waiting for us with a list of complaints and a plate of hot kachoris. That is the kind of opportunity this journey is about: a real business, a real problem, and a small fix that saves hours.

But the story I am writing about today came from someone else in that workshop. Dinesh's brother-in-law, Alok, had arrived from Patna the day before to take back a consignment of dhoop for his own shop. Alok runs a small electrical contracting firm in Patna, wiring shops and houses, and he is the kind of man who reads every newspaper in the house before breakfast.

When Kaka introduced me as someone who writes about business frauds, Alok put down his kachori and said, "Then you must write about the tower man."

## Fifteen years of trust and the Patna mobile tower fraud

The case he meant was reported in [Prabhat Khabar from Patna](https://www.prabhatkhabar.com/state/bihar/patna/mobile-tower-installation-scam-56-lakh), and he showed me the report on his phone while the women at the next table kept rolling sticks without missing a beat.

According to that report, a businessman named Indrajeet Kumar, a resident of East Indira Nagar in Kankarbagh, has filed an FIR at the Patrakar Nagar police station alleging that he was cheated of ₹56 lakh in the name of a mobile tower installation business.

The accused, according to the complaint, is a man from the Hanuman Nagar area whom the businessman had known for about fifteen years.

The report says the accused approached him with news that he had received a large contract for installing mobile towers in Odisha, and persuaded him to invest in that business with the promise of heavy profit.

Because of the old relationship and the trust between them, the businessman paid a total of ₹56 lakh in different instalments.

After a long time, he received neither profit nor his principal, and when he asked for the money back, the accused began to make excuses.

The police have registered the case and, according to the report, are proceeding on the basis of technical evidence and call details. All of this is an allegation at the stage of investigation, and the accused will have his chance to answer it.

Alok did not know either man personally, but he knew the area, and he knew the type of offer very well. "In Patna, everybody has a cousin with a contract," he said, and the women at the next table laughed because they clearly knew a cousin like that too.

Kaka laughed as well, and then said something that made the laughter settle. "The contract is never the problem," he said. "The problem is the old friendship that stops you from asking to see it." That, to me, is the heart of this patna mobile tower fraud case and many others like it. The fraud does not depend on technology or on a clever app.

It depends on an old acquaintance fraud pattern as ancient as trade itself: a trusted person, a big opportunity far away, and a request for money now.

## How a real telecom infrastructure contract works

Alok has done subcontract wiring work for tower sites in Bihar, so he knows how this business actually runs, and he explained it with the patience of a man who has done the paperwork himself. A mobile tower installation project starts with the telecom operator or a large tower infrastructure company deciding where it needs coverage.

[IMAGE 2]

That company issues work to established contractors through tenders, purchase orders or formal work orders, each with a reference number, a scope of work, site addresses, timelines and payment terms. The main contractor then hires smaller subcontractors for civil foundations, steel erection, electrical work and power backup.

At every level, there are documents: the work order, the bill of quantities, the measurement records, the invoices and the payments, which usually come after work is done and verified, often with a delay of weeks or months. "Nobody in this business gets paid first," Alok said. "Everybody waits. That is why contractors always need working capital." This point matters a great deal.

A genuine telecom infrastructure contract does create a need for money, because the contractor has to buy steel, cement and cable and pay labour before the payment arrives. So the request for investment in a tower contract investment offer is not automatically false.

The question is whether the contract exists, whether the person asking for money is actually the contractor, and whether your money is protected by anything more than friendship. Kaka compared it to the way the big temple festivals in Kashi are organised. "Every year, someone collects money for decorations and bhandara," he said.

"The honest organiser shows the receipts on a board near the temple. The dishonest one shows only his smile."

## Who pays whom in an Odisha tower project, and when

I asked Alok to draw the payment flow on the back of one of Dinesh's order sheets, and the table below is what we wrote together.

| Step in a genuine tower project | Who pays | What document exists | What the fake offer usually shows instead |
|---|---|---|---|
| Operator or tower company awards work | No payment from you | Signed work order or purchase order with a reference number | A verbal claim of "a big contract in another state" |
| Contractor arranges materials and labour | Contractor uses working capital or a bank credit line | Supplier invoices, bank loan papers | A request for personal investment at high profit |
| Work done and measured on site | Nobody yet | Measurement book, site photographs, completion certificate | No site address you can visit |
| Contractor raises bill | Tower company processes it | Invoice with GST, approval record | No invoices, only promises of "payment soon" |
| Payment released | Tower company pays contractor's business account | Bank credit with company name | Excuses, delays, new reasons to pay more |

The odisha tower project in this case, according to the report, was never shown to the businessman in any such form that led to returns. A careful investor in that position would have asked for the work order, the site list and the business bank account, and would have checked at least one of them independently. I say this with no wish to blame the businessman in Patna.

He trusted someone he had known for fifteen years, and most of us would do the same. That is exactly why the checks need to become a habit that we follow even with the people we trust most.

## Why investment in instalments makes a loss bigger

Alok pointed out one line in the report that he thought was the most important: the money was paid in different instalments. This is a pattern I have seen in many cases, and I want to explain why it is so dangerous. The first instalment is usually small and comes with a convincing story.

[IMAGE 5]

Then a second request arrives, often with a reason: the steel price has gone up, the site needs a deposit, the payment from the company is about to come but a fee must be paid first. Each new instalment feels smaller than the risk of losing what has already been paid.

Psychologists call this the sunk cost effect, but in our homes we have always known it as throwing good money after bad.

Investment in instalments, without a document that grows with each payment, means that by the time you feel doubt, you have already paid most of the amount. Kaka told a story from the ghats that fits this perfectly.

A pilgrim once came to him in tears because a man near one of the temples had asked him for a small amount for a special puja, then a little more for flowers, then more for a "special priest", and finally a large sum for a ritual that would "complete" the others.

"Every step was small," Kaka said. "Together it was his whole travel money. I took him to the temple office, and they told him that the real puja costs very little and needs no special man." The lesson for business is the same.

Decide the total amount you are willing to risk before the first payment, write it down, and stop there no matter what new reason arrives.

## Tower company verification in one afternoon

On the drive back from the workshop, Kaka asked me to make a simple list he could share with the many small businessmen he knows in Kashi, several of whom receive offers like this every year. Here is the tower company verification list I gave him, and every step can be done in an afternoon.

[IMAGE 4]

Ask for the work order or purchase order, in writing, with the issuing company's name, the reference number and the list of sites.

Call the issuing company's official office, using a number from its own website rather than one given by the person asking for money, and confirm that the work order exists and that the person is its contractor. Search the contractor's company or firm in official records, check its GST registration and ask for the last six months of its business bank statements.

Ask to visit at least one active site, even if it is in another state; a genuine contractor will be proud to show you work in progress.

Put any investment into the firm's business account, against a written agreement that states the amount, the project, the share of profit and the date of return.

For readers who own buildings and receive the more common version of this offer, a rooftop tower with "monthly rent", the rule is even simpler: genuine tower companies never ask the owner to pay an advance or a registration fee.

The Department of Telecommunications runs the [Tarang Sanchar portal](https://tarangsanchar.gov.in/), where the public can see information about mobile towers and the safety norms that apply to them, and it is a good place to start before believing any offer that arrives by phone or letter. Work order verification takes a few phone calls. Recovering money after an FIR takes years.

## What Dinesh's workshop taught us about work order verification

After Alok finished, Dinesh took us around his workshop, and I saw something that made the whole morning feel connected.

[IMAGE 3]

Dinesh has one large buyer, a wholesaler who supplies incense to temple shops across the city, and Dinesh works with him on a written order every month. The order lists the quantity, the fragrance, the price per box, the delivery date and the payment date, and both men sign it.

"He is like my elder brother," Dinesh said, "but we still sign. Because when he forgets, the paper remembers." That is the same principle that would have protected the businessman in Patna.

Dinesh's real problem was a different one, and it is the kind of problem many small manufacturers have. Smaller shops order by phone, and those orders are never written down properly, so he often delivers without a record and then cannot remember who owes him what.

Kaka and I sat with him for an hour and set up a very simple system: every phone order goes into a shared sheet from his phone, a short message goes back to the shop confirming the order, and a weekly reminder goes to anyone with an unpaid balance.

This is the kind of practical work I have described in [late payment automation](https://ethicalfounder.in/late-payment-automation/) and [customer service automation](https://ethicalfounder.in/customer-service-automation/), and it costs Dinesh almost nothing.

He was so pleased that he insisted on sending a box of his best sandalwood dhoop to every temple Kaka visits this month.

For anyone thinking of starting something similar, I have written about [small-town business ideas](https://ethicalfounder.in/business-ideas-for-small-towns/) and about [testing an idea before spending](https://ethicalfounder.in/business-ideas-from-a-family-complaint-test-before-spending/), and Dinesh's workshop is a living example of both: a simple product, steady local demand, and growth that comes from doing the work well.

The women in the workshop, Kaka told me, have been working with Dinesh for years, and several of them have educated their children on these earnings. That is what a real business does over time. It feeds people. A false contract feeds only the person who invented it.

## The mobile tower installation offers that reach small towns

While Dinesh packed the dhoop boxes for Alok's shop, Alok told us about the other versions of this offer that he sees in Patna and in the towns around it.

The most common, he said, is the rooftop offer: a letter or a phone call telling a house owner or shop owner that his building has been "selected" for mobile tower installation, with a monthly rent of a very attractive amount, followed by a request for a registration fee, a stamp paper fee, an NOC fee and so on.

The second is the franchise-style offer, where a person is told he can become an "authorised tower partner" for a district if he pays a security deposit. The third is the kind in the Patna report, where a known person claims to hold a large contract and asks for investment.

All three share the same shape as the fake dealership and franchise offers I have written about in [dealership fraud ads using real brand names](https://ethicalfounder.in/dealership-fraud-ads-tata-ather-mg/) and [franchise fraud with real brand names](https://ethicalfounder.in/franchise-fraud-real-brand-names-checks/): a famous industry, a confident story, and money that must move before any work begins.

Alok said the letters have become far more convincing in the last couple of years. "Earlier the spelling was bad," he said. "Now the letters look better than the real companies' letters. Logos, signatures, stamps, everything."

That is partly because designing a convincing document now takes minutes with AI tools, which is one reason India is tightening rules on synthetic content, as I explained in [AI labelling rules under the IT Rules amendment](https://ethicalfounder.in/ai-labelling-rules-in-india-it-rules-amendment/).

A beautiful letter proves only that someone owns a design tool. Kaka listened and added a Kashi example, as he always does. "Every year someone prints a beautiful pass for VIP darshan and sells it to pilgrims outside the station," he said.

"The pass is perfect. The temple has never heard of it." For a small business owner, the safest habit is to treat every mobile tower installation letter, work order or partnership offer as unverified until you have confirmed it through the company's own official channel.

The same habit protects you from the [trading apps that show profits you cannot withdraw](https://ethicalfounder.in/fake-trading-app-profits-cant-withdraw/), because the method is always the same: a convincing screen or paper in front, and nothing real behind it.

## What happens after a business fraud FIR in Patna

Alok asked me what the businessman in Patna can realistically expect now, and it is a fair question for anyone in the same position.

Once an FIR is registered for cheating, the police investigate the bank transfers, the call records and any documents exchanged, which is what the report says is happening in this business fraud fir patna case.

If the money moved through bank accounts, the trail can often show where it went, and the police can seek to freeze accounts or attach property linked to the accused.

The complainant should keep a single file with every transfer receipt, every message and a written timeline, and should follow up with the investigating officer in writing at regular intervals.

If the accused claimed an odisha tower project, the police may also verify with the companies concerned whether any such contract ever existed, and that verification becomes strong evidence either way. A civil suit for recovery can run alongside the criminal case, and a lawyer can advise whether to file one. None of this is fast, and I will not pretend otherwise.

But a well-documented complaint is the strongest position a cheated investor can be in, and it is also a warning to others in the same circle who might have been approached next. Alok said he would share the report and these steps with the traders' group in his area.

That, Kaka said, is the real use of bad news: it should travel faster than the next offer.

I have seen the same in my own writing, where [word of mouth carries a warning](https://ethicalfounder.in/word-of-mouth-vs-ads-for-a-first-year-business/) much further than any official notice. Rising costs push people toward such offers too, and when fuel, cement and steel all move together, as they have recently, contractors feel the squeeze first, which I explained in [what fuel price changes do to small businesses](https://ethicalfounder.in/iran-war-fuel-prices-small-businesses-india/). A contractor under pressure is exactly the person a false opportunity is designed to reach.

## Old acquaintance fraud and saying no to an old friend

At lunch, which Dinesh's wife served on the workshop floor on banana leaves, Alok asked the question that I think every reader of this story will have. "If your oldest friend comes with a contract and asks for money, what do you say without hurting him?" I told him what I tell myself.

I say that I am happy to help, and that I need three things first: the work order, a visit to a site, and a written agreement into the business account.

If the friend is honest, he will understand, and many honest contractors will even be relieved, because the documents protect them too. If he is offended, his offence tells me something I needed to know. I also suggested a gentler alternative that has saved many relationships in my own circle. Instead of investing, offer to help your friend approach a bank for working capital against the work order.

A genuine contract with a reputed company is often good security for a bank, and I have explained the process in [business ideas that need a bank loan](https://ethicalfounder.in/business-ideas-that-need-a-bank-loan/). If the friend refuses to go to a bank and insists on your personal money, that refusal is the answer.

For small business owners who are building their first capital, I would also suggest reading my notes on [money in the first six months of a small business](https://ethicalfounder.in/money-in-the-first-six-months-of-a-small-business/), because the habits that protect a new business from bad investors also protect it from bad investments.

Kaka listened to all this quietly, and then said, "In Kashi we say that Shiva watches the friendship, and Yama watches the account. Keep both clean."

Alok repeated the line twice so that he would remember it for his own friends in Patna. And if, despite all this, money has already gone, the way forward is the same one the businessman in Patna has taken: a written complaint, every transfer record in order, and a business fraud fir patna police can act on with call details and bank trails. It is slow, but it is the right path.

## The smoke that rises to Shiva

In the evening, back near the ghats, Kaka and I lit two sticks of Dinesh's sandalwood incense at a small Shiva shrine near the steps of Harishchandra Ghat. The smoke rose straight up in the still air for a moment and then drifted toward the river. Kaka said that incense is offered to Shiva because it gives itself completely, burning down to nothing while it fills the space with fragrance.

[IMAGE 6]

"It does not ask for anything back," he said. "That is why it is pure." I have been thinking about that since last night, sitting on the steps with the fires of the cremation ground in front of me. A business deal is the opposite of an offering, and it should be.

In a deal, both sides are supposed to receive something, and the papers are there to make sure they do.

When we confuse the two, when we give money to a friend's contract the way we give incense to a shrine, without asking for anything back except trust, we turn a business into a prayer, and a prayer cannot be enforced in court.

The karma of the person who deceives will find him in its own time, and I believe that firmly. But the karma of the rest of us includes the duty to protect what we have earned through honest work, because it belongs to our families as much as to us. Kashi teaches that nothing material goes with us at the end.

It also teaches, through Harishchandra's story, that a word kept is the one thing that never burns. Ask for the work order. Keep your word, and ask others to keep theirs in writing.

Tomorrow Kaka wants to take me to a small supermarket owner in the city who has been receiving franchise offers that sound too good, and I suspect that will be the next story.

## Questions People Ask About Mobile Tower Installation

**Is investing in a mobile tower installation contract safe?**

It can be safe only when there is a verified work order from a real telecom or tower company, the person asking is the actual contractor, and your money goes into the business account against a written agreement.

**What happened in the Patna mobile tower fraud case?**

According to a Prabhat Khabar report, a Kankarbagh businessman alleged that an acquaintance of about fifteen years took ₹56 lakh in instalments for a claimed mobile tower contract in Odisha, gave neither profit nor principal, and an FIR was registered at Patrakar Nagar police station.

**How does a real telecom infrastructure contract work?**

The telecom or tower company issues a formal work order to a contractor, who completes the work, raises an invoice and is paid into the business account after verification, usually with some delay.

**How do I do tower company verification?**

Ask for the written work order, call the issuing company on its official number to confirm it, check the contractor's registrations and bank statements, and visit an active site before investing.

**Do genuine companies charge a fee to install a tower on my roof?**

No, genuine tower companies do not ask building owners to pay an advance or registration fee to install a tower, so any such demand is a warning sign.

**What is work order verification?**

It is the process of confirming with the issuing company that a work order, its reference number, its sites and its contractor are real before you put money into the project.

**Why is investment in instalments risky?**

Each new instalment feels smaller than the fear of losing what you already paid, so people keep paying without new documents, and the total loss grows larger before doubt sets in.

**How do I recognise old acquaintance fraud?**

Watch for a trusted person bringing a large opportunity in a distant place, refusing to share documents, asking for personal money instead of a bank loan, and offering high profit with no written terms.

**What should I do if I have already paid for a fake tower contract investment?**

Collect every transfer record, message and call log, send a written demand, and file a police complaint for cheating, as police can trace bank trails and call details.

**Where can I learn about mobile towers in my area?**

The Department of Telecommunications' Tarang Sanchar portal provides public information about mobile towers and the safety norms that apply to them.
