# The small business owners profit margin gap: why most can't name their own number

Yesterday I wrote about the loan mistake that sinks first-time
founders, the one where the instalment gets sized to the sanction
letter instead of to what the business has proven it can earn. If you
haven't read it, go and look, because today's piece sits right
underneath it. Several of you wrote back with the same question in
different words: "How do I even know what my business earns?" That
question deserves its own answer. And tomorrow I'm taking on the
paperwork that confuses almost every first-time founder, GST and
Udyam, side by side. Some of you are going to find out you've been
worrying about the wrong one.

## The small business owners profit margin gap starts with a simple question

When I sit with a shop owner or a small manufacturer for the first
time, I usually ask one question early in the conversation. "On a
hundred rupees of sales, how much do you keep?"

The answers tell me more than any balance sheet. Some owners give a
confident figure that turns out to be the markup on one product. Some
give the number their supplier told them years ago. Some say "it
depends," which is true, and then can't say on what. A few look at me
as if the question doesn't apply to them, because the business has
always managed to pay the bills and that has felt like enough.

I want to be careful here, because I'm not making fun of anyone. These
are often hardworking, sharp people who know their customers by name,
can spot a bad batch of stock from across the room, and have kept a
business alive through things that would break most corporate
managers. The gap is narrow and specific. They know their business in
every way except one: the arithmetic of what each sale leaves behind.

That gap matters more than it seems. The small business owners profit
margin question is the thing that decides whether you can afford a
loan, whether a discount is smart or ruinous, whether a new product
line is worth adding, and whether the long hours are building
something or only keeping it standing. Without the number, every one
of those decisions is a guess.

I remember a hardware shop owner, years into running a busy counter,
who did the exercise with me over two evenings. He was certain his
business made a comfortable living. When we finished, the shop was
earning less than he paid his senior helper, once his own hours were
counted at a fair rate. He sat back and laughed, a tired laugh, and
said he'd suspected it for a while and never wanted to check. Within a
few months he'd cut two product lines, renegotiated with one supplier
and raised prices on the items customers never questioned. Everything
he needed was already inside the business he had. The number had just
never been brought out into the light.

## Why this happens even to careful, hardworking owners

There are a few reasons the number stays hidden, and none of them has
to do with intelligence.

### Cash in the drawer feels like profit

In a small business, the clearest daily signal is the cash at the end
of the day and the balance in the account. When that number is
healthy, it feels like the business is making money. The trouble is
that the drawer holds everyone's money mixed together: the supplier
you haven't paid yet, the GST you've collected for the government, the
rent due next week, and somewhere in there, your profit. A full drawer
can sit on top of a loss for months before the supplier bills catch
up.

### Markup gets mistaken for margin

This is the single most common arithmetic slip I see. An owner buys
something for ₹100 and sells it for ₹125, and says the margin is 25
percent. That 25 percent is the markup, calculated on cost. The margin
is calculated on the selling price: ₹25 kept out of ₹125 sold, which
is 20 percent. The difference sounds small, and across a year of
pricing decisions it adds up to a lot of money that the owner thought
was there and wasn't. Profit margin mistakes in retail very often
start right here, with a shop owner who prices every product using
markup and reads the result as margin.

### Personal and business money live in one pocket

When the owner's household expenses, the children's school fees and
the shop's stock purchases all come out of the same account, there's
no clean way to see what the business made. Many small owners also
don't pay themselves a salary, so their own time never appears as a
cost. The business looks profitable partly because the owner is
working for free.

### Small costs never get counted

Rent and stock are easy to remember. The packaging, the delivery fuel,
the mobile recharge used for orders, the payment gateway charges, the
tea and snacks for the loading crew, the breakage, the stock that
expired on the shelf: those slip through. Each one is small. Together
they can be the difference between a thin margin and no margin.

### Nothing forces the calculation

A salaried manager has reports and targets. A small business owner has
nobody asking. If the bank isn't asking, the tax return is filed on a
simplified basis, and the family is fed, there's no moment that forces
the number into the open. Small business financial literacy grows when
something forces the question, and for many owners, that moment only
comes with a crisis.

### Margin advice gets borrowed from other businesses

A lot of owners carry a margin figure in their head that came from
somewhere else: a cousin in a different trade, a supplier's sales
pitch, a video about a business in another city. People ask me
constantly for the right profit margin by business type India-wide, as
if there's a chart somewhere that settles it. I haven't found a
published figure I'd trust for a specific shop, and I'd be wary of
anyone who offers one with confidence. Two sweet shops on the same
road can run on very different margins because one owns the building
and the other pays a heavy rent.

Borrowed numbers do real damage. An owner who believes their trade
"always" makes a certain margin stops checking their own. When the
real figure drifts below the borrowed one, nobody notices until the
account is empty. The only margin that tells you anything is the one
you calculate from your own sales and your own costs.

### Why small businesses underprice their work

I've thought a lot about why small businesses underprice their work,
because I see it everywhere, from tailors and tutors to fabricators
and caterers. A few patterns come up again and again.

The first is fear. A new owner worries that a higher price will drive
customers away, so they start low to win the first orders. The first
orders come, the low price becomes the customer's expectation, and
raising it later feels like a betrayal. Starting low is easy. Climbing
back up is very hard.

The second is that skilled work feels easy to the person doing it. The
tailor who can finish a blouse in a couple of hours forgets that the
customer couldn't do it in a week. Owners price their own skill at
what it costs them in effort, when they should price it at what it's
worth to the buyer.

The third is the missing salary we talked about. When the owner's time
is invisible, the business seems profitable at a price that's really
paying the owner far less than they'd earn working for someone else.

The fourth is plain arithmetic, the markup and margin mix-up from
earlier. An owner who believes they're keeping 25 percent when they're
keeping 20 percent will underprice every product by the same quiet
amount for years.

If any of this sounds familiar, you're in good company. Fixing it
rarely means a dramatic price rise overnight. It usually means small,
steady increases on new orders, clearer pricing for custom work, and a
new rule that every price gets checked against the margin before it's
quoted.

## How to calculate profit margin for small business, done simply

You can do this with a notebook, a calculator and an evening. I'll
walk through it the way I do it with owners across a table.

### Step one: separate the three kinds of money

Before any calculation, sort every rupee that goes out of the business
into three groups.

- **Direct costs.** Money spent on the specific things you sold:
  stock, raw materials, the fabric for the blouse, the flour for the
  cake, the courier charge for a particular order.
- **Running costs.** Money you spend whether or not you sell anything
  this month: rent, salaries, electricity, internet, loan instalments'
  interest portion, licence renewals.
- **Money that was never yours.** GST collected from customers if
  you're registered, and any other tax you collect on behalf of the
  government. This goes out of your revenue figure before you
  calculate anything.

That third group catches people out. If you're registered under GST
and you collect tax on your sales, that tax belongs to the government,
and you'll account for it through the returns process on the [GST
portal](https://www.gst.gov.in/). Counting it as sales makes your
margin look better than it is.

### Step two: gross margin

Take your sales for the month, net of GST collected. Subtract your
direct costs. What's left is your gross profit, and gross profit
divided by sales is your gross margin.

### Step three: net margin

Take your gross profit and subtract every running cost, including a
fair salary for yourself even if you don't draw one. What's left is
your net profit. Net profit divided by sales is your net margin.

### Gross margin vs net margin explained simply

Here's gross margin vs net margin explained simply, the way I'd
explain it to my own cousin. Gross margin tells you whether each sale
is a good sale. Net margin tells you whether the whole business is a
good business. A shop can have a healthy gross margin on every product
and still lose money overall, because the rent is too high for the
volume it sells. A shop can also have a thin gross margin and do well,
because it sells a huge volume with very low running costs. You need
both numbers. Each one, on its own, will mislead you.

### A worked example across three small business types

These figures are illustrations, chosen to show how the arithmetic
works in three very different businesses. They are not industry
averages and they don't describe any real shop.

| Line | Neighbourhood grocery shop | Home tailoring service | Home bakery taking orders |
|---|---|---|---|
| Monthly sales (net of GST) | ₹6,00,000 | ₹90,000 | ₹1,20,000 |
| Direct costs | ₹5,10,000 (stock) | ₹18,000 (thread, lining, buttons, small fabric) | ₹54,000 (ingredients, boxes, delivery) |
| Gross profit | ₹90,000 | ₹72,000 | ₹66,000 |
| Gross margin | 15% | 80% | 55% |
| Running costs | ₹45,000 (rent, helper, power) | ₹12,000 (power, machine upkeep, phone) | ₹14,000 (gas, power, phone, platform fees) |
| Owner's fair salary | ₹30,000 | ₹40,000 | ₹35,000 |
| Net profit after owner's salary | ₹15,000 | ₹20,000 | ₹17,000 |
| Net margin | 2.5% | 22.2% | 14.2% |

Look at what this table shows. The grocery shop moves the most money
by far, and its net margin is the thinnest. Every rupee of discount,
every expired packet, every rupee of extra rent comes straight out of
a very small number. The tailoring service has a high gross margin
because most of what the customer pays for is skill and time, and the
risk there is that the owner's own salary gets forgotten. The bakery
sits in the middle, and its margin is very sensitive to ingredient
prices and to how many orders get wasted or remade.

None of these figures should be read as the profit margin by business
type India-wide. Real margins swing widely with location, rent,
supplier terms, the owner's pricing and the competition on the street.
What the table teaches is the method, and the method works for any
business.

### Understanding unit economics small business owners can use

Once you have the monthly picture, go one level down and look at a
single sale. What does one kurta, one cake, one service visit cost you
directly, and what do you get for it? This is understanding unit
economics small business owners can use every day, without any jargon.
When you know that one cake leaves ₹220 after ingredients and box, you
can decide in seconds whether a ₹100 discount for a bulk order makes
sense, or how many cakes a month cover the gas and power.

### The break even point for a small business

The break even point for a small business is the level of sales at
which the gross profit exactly covers the running costs and your
salary. Below it, you're losing money. Above it, you're making some.
The arithmetic is simple: divide your monthly running costs plus your
salary by your gross margin.

Take the bakery from the table. Running costs plus salary are ₹49,000.
Gross margin is 55 percent. So the bakery breaks even at about ₹89,000
of monthly sales. Every month above that, it earns. Every month below
that, it eats into savings. Knowing that one figure changes how the
owner looks at a slow week. It turns "business is down" into "we're
about ₹15,000 of sales short of break-even this month, and we know
exactly what that gap looks like in orders."

### Step four: repeat it every month and compare

Doing the calculation once is useful. Doing it every month is where
the value really builds. Keep the same page format, fill it in at the
end of each month, and put the months side by side. Patterns appear
quickly: the month when gross margin dipped because a supplier raised
prices, the month when net margin jumped because you dropped a slow
product, the festival month that looked busy and earned less than
expected because of heavy discounts.

This is how to calculate profit margin for small business owners in a
way that sticks. One-off exercises get forgotten. A monthly page
becomes a habit, and after a few months you'll find you can estimate
your margin on a new product before you've stocked it. That instinct
is worth more than any software, though a simple spreadsheet helps
once the notebook gets crowded.

If your business has distinct parts, a counter and a delivery service,
or retail and wholesale customers, calculate them separately once a
quarter. Combined figures hide a weak part behind a strong one.

## What changes once you know your number

The calculation itself takes an evening. What follows from it can
change the next five years of the business. These are the changes I've
watched happen most often, in my consulting work and in the free
sessions I run for owners who can't afford to pay a consultant.

### Pricing stops being a guess

The most immediate change is in pricing. Many owners price by looking
at the shop next door and charging a little less. Once they know their
margin, they can see whether that price leaves them anything at all.
Pricing mistakes from not knowing margin are the most expensive
mistakes on this list, because they repeat on every single sale.

### Discounts get chosen instead of given away

A discount that feels generous at the counter looks very different on
paper. On the grocery shop in the table, a 5 percent discount takes
away a third of the gross margin on that sale. On the tailoring
service, it barely moves the number. Owners who know their margins
give discounts where they can afford them and politely hold firm where
they can't.

### The owner's time gets a value

When you put your own fair salary into the calculation, you see
clearly how much of the business's profit is really your wages. That's
a hard thing to see. It's also the moment a lot of owners decide to
raise prices, cut a product line that eats their evenings, or hire
help for the parts of the work that don't need them.

### Product lines get judged on what they earn

Every shop has products that sell well and earn little, and products
that sell slowly and earn a lot. Without margins, owners judge
products by how fast they move, and they keep restocking items that
bring footfall but leave almost nothing behind. Once you know the
margin on each line, you can make deliberate choices. Keep the
low-margin item that brings customers in, if those customers buy other
things. Drop the one that only ties up money and shelf space.

The same thinking applies to services. A tailor who calculates margin
per job often discovers that alterations, which feel like small easy
money, take more time per rupee than full stitching orders. Pricing
mistakes from not knowing margin hide in exactly these small, frequent
jobs, because nobody ever sits down and times them.

### Borrowing gets sized properly

This connects straight back to yesterday's piece. You can't size a
loan to proven margin if you don't know your margin. Owners who've
done this exercise walk into the bank with a page that shows what the
business can carry, and that page gets them better loans and fewer
sleepless nights. Lenders like SIDBI publish their MSME programmes on
the [SIDBI website](https://www.sidbi.in/en/), and every one of them
will look more closely at your margins than at your sales.

### Saying no gets easier

Owners who don't know their margin find it very hard to turn down
work. Every order looks like money, so they accept the bulk order at a
cut price, the custom job with endless changes and the customer who
always pays late. Once the margin is on paper, some of those orders
show up for what they are: work that costs more than it earns. I've
watched owners grow calmer and more profitable in the same year simply
because they started declining the wrong orders politely and spending
the freed-up time on the right ones.

It also changes how you talk to customers. When a buyer asks for a
lower price, you can answer with a clear reason, a smaller quantity, a
simpler design or a longer delivery date, instead of either caving or
refusing. Customers respect an owner who knows their numbers, and many
of them will meet you halfway.

### Tax and margin stop getting confused

Here's a confusion I meet often. Many small owners file income tax
under the presumptive scheme in section 44AD, which lets eligible
small businesses declare a set percentage of turnover as income
instead of keeping full accounts. The details and current limits are
on the [Income Tax Department's e-filing
portal](https://www.incometax.gov.in/iec/foportal/). Some owners then
start believing that the presumptive percentage is their actual profit
margin. That percentage was designed as a simplified figure for tax
purposes and says nothing about your own costs. Your real margin could
be higher or much lower, and only your own calculation will tell you
which.

## Where small business owners profit margin gets quietly eaten

Margin rarely vanishes in one dramatic event. It leaks, a little at a
time, through gaps that nobody is watching. These are the leaks I
check first when an owner tells me the business is busy but the money
never seems to be there.

### Wastage and shrinkage

Stock that expires, breaks, goes missing or gets eaten by staff is a
direct cost with nothing sold against it. In food businesses, it's
often the largest leak. Track it for one month by writing down
everything thrown away or unaccounted for, and the total will surprise
you.

### Credit to customers

Every sale on credit is money you've spent on stock that you haven't
received yet. Some of it never comes back. And even the part that does
come back costs you, because you've had to fund the gap from your own
pocket or a loan. Owners who are generous with credit often have
healthy margins on paper and empty accounts in practice.

The fix starts with a proper credit ledger: every customer who owes
you, the amount, the date of the sale and the date they promised to
pay. Look at it every Saturday. Anyone more than a month overdue gets
a polite call, and nobody gets fresh credit while an old amount is
still open. It sounds strict, and most regular customers accept it
once they see you apply it to everyone. The owners who keep that
ledger tend to recover far more of what they're owed, and they stop
funding their customers' cash flow out of their own margin.

### Supplier prices that drift upward

Suppliers raise prices in small steps. If your selling price doesn't
move with them, your margin shrinks without anyone deciding to shrink
it. Check your top ten purchase prices every quarter against what they
were a year ago. For small manufacturers buying raw material, it's
also worth knowing that [NSIC](https://www.nsic.co.in/) runs support
schemes for micro and small enterprises, including help with raw
material procurement, which can be one way to steady your input costs.

### Payment and platform fees

Card machines, payment gateways, delivery apps and online marketplaces
all take a cut. Each fee is small, and each one comes out of margin,
not revenue. If a growing share of your sales comes through a
platform, recalculate your margin for those sales separately. Some
owners find that their busiest channel is their least profitable.

### Free extras that became standard

The free delivery that started as a favour for one customer and became
expected by everyone. The extra alteration that was never charged. The
bonus sweet in every box. Generosity is good business up to a point.
Past that point, it's a price cut you never decided to make.

### Owner's time on the wrong work

When the owner spends hours on tasks a helper could do, the business
pays the owner's rate for helper work. That doesn't show up on any
receipt, but it shows up in what the business could have earned if the
owner's time went to selling, pricing and finding better suppliers.

### Interest and slow-moving stock

Two costs hide especially well. The first is interest on any loan or
overdraft used to buy stock. If you borrowed to fill the shelves, the
interest is part of what that stock really cost you, and a product
that sits for months carries months of interest. The second is the
cost of slow stock itself: money locked on a shelf can't buy the
fast-moving items customers are asking for. Owners who look at how
long each product sits before it sells often find that their slowest
lines are quietly eating the margin their best lines earn.

A simple fix is to mark the purchase month on every carton or batch
when it comes in. At month-end, walk the shelves and note anything
older than three months. Those items need a decision, a markdown, a
return to the supplier or a different place on the shelf, before they
cost you more.

### Profit margin mistakes in retail and services, side by side

Retail and service businesses tend to make different mistakes, and it
helps to see them next to each other.

Retail owners, the grocery and general store and garment shop owners,
usually work on thin gross margins and high volume. Their most common
profit margin mistakes in retail are pricing by markup instead of
margin, ignoring wastage, giving too much credit and not noticing
supplier price drift. For them, a small leak is a large share of a
small margin.

Service owners, the tailors, tutors, repair shops and beauticians,
usually have high gross margins and low volume. Their most common
mistakes are forgetting their own salary, underpricing their skill,
and giving away extras that should be charged. For them, the margin
looks fine until they count their own time.

Both groups benefit from the same habit: a monthly page with sales,
direct costs, running costs and their own fair salary, worked out to
two percentages. It takes less time than most owners spend arguing
with a single difficult supplier.

## A closing word on knowing your own numbers

Small business financial literacy comes from habits more than degrees:
separating three kinds of money, counting the small costs, and having
the honesty to pay yourself on paper even when you don't in cash. The
Ministry of MSME runs entrepreneurship and skill programmes listed on
the [MSME ministry's site](https://msme.gov.in/), and some owners find
a short course helps them build the habit. Many owners build it on
their own with a notebook and an hour every month-end. I've seen a
vegetable vendor keep cleaner margin notes than a factory owner with a
full-time accountant, because the vendor cared about the number and
the factory owner had handed it to someone else.

Do the calculation for last month, this week. Write down your gross
margin, your net margin and your break-even sales figure. Keep the
page where you'll see it before you quote your next price, give your
next discount or sign your next loan. That's all. Your business will
be the same business tomorrow, and you'll be running it with the one
number you were missing.
