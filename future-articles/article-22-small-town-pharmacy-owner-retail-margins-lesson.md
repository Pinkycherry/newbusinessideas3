# Small town pharmacy business lessons: what one owner taught me about retail margins

Last Sunday I took my coffee out to the balcony early, before the street woke up, and found myself thinking about a conversation I'd had months ago and never quite finished processing. A pharmacy owner in a small town had told me, almost in passing, that he did not decide the price of a large part of what sat on his shelves. The words came out the way you might mention the weather. I had nodded at the time. On the balcony, with the cup going cold in my hand, I realised that one sentence had quietly changed how I explain margins to every retailer I work with.

Yesterday's piece was about business ideas that do better in a small town than in a metro. This one goes inside a business that fits that description exactly. A chemist shop is about as steady a small-town business as exists, and the man who ran this one understood retail economics better than most people I've met with business degrees. I'll share the lessons from a real small business owner who never meant to teach anyone, what the rules around medicine pricing say, and what any retailer, pharmacy or otherwise, can take from it.

## The conversation that started this

I had gone to the town to help a relative of his with something unrelated, a small paperwork problem, the sort of favour I do when a family can't stretch to a consultant's fee. The pharmacy was on the main road near the bus stand, and while I waited for a document to be printed nearby, I ended up sitting on the stool beside his counter for most of an afternoon.

I have changed enough details that the shop and the man can't be identified. He asked for that, and it costs the lessons nothing. I'll also say clearly that he never shared his own numbers with me, and I won't invent any on his behalf. Where I use figures here, they are either from the official rules or they are illustrations, labelled as such, with arithmetic you can check yourself.

What struck me first was the rhythm of the counter. A mother came in for a child's fever syrup. An older man came in for his monthly blood pressure tablets and asked the owner whether a new pain balm was safe to use alongside them. A young woman wanted a pregnancy test and said very little. A farmer's son came in with a crumpled prescription and asked whether there was a cheaper version of one of the medicines. A delivery boy from a distributor dropped off two cartons and took away a box of returns. Between customers, the owner checked a register, made a note in a small book of credit, and glanced at a refrigerator in the corner.

Somewhere in the second hour I asked him the question I ask every shopkeeper: where does the money come from? He laughed and said that in a pharmacy, the more useful thing to watch was where the money leaks out. Then he told me that he didn't decide the price of much of his shelf, and that once you accept that, the whole business becomes a question of what you do control.

I've thought about that afternoon more than almost any meeting with a paying client.

## What margin actually looked like in that business

To understand what he meant, you have to understand how medicine prices are set in India, because the rules shape the margin of every chemist shop in the country, and they decide which pharmacy business ideas in India are worth pursuing at all.

### The rules a pharmacy owner can't change

Medicine prices in India are governed by the Drugs (Prices Control) Order, 2013, usually called the DPCO. The Department of Pharmaceuticals publishes the [consolidated text of the DPCO 2013](https://www.pharma-dept.gov.in/sites/default/files/DPCO%202013.pdf) with its amendments.

The order splits medicines into two groups. Scheduled formulations are the essential medicines listed in its first schedule, drawn from the National List of Essential Medicines. For these, the government fixes a ceiling price. The formula in the order is direct: it takes the average price to retailer of the medicine across brands with at least one percent market share, and adds a margin to retailer, which the order sets at 16 percent. In plain language, the ceiling price of a scheduled medicine is the average price to retailer plus sixteen percent of that price. Local taxes are added on top to arrive at the maximum retail price.

Because the ceiling is built on an average, the margin a particular shop earns on a particular brand depends on what it paid. Buy a brand whose price to retailer sits below that average and the shop keeps more than sixteen percent when it sells at the ceiling. Buy one priced above the average and it keeps less. The order caps what the customer pays, and the chemist's slice moves with the purchase price.

Non-scheduled formulations, everything outside that list, have no ceiling price and no fixed retailer margin under the order. Manufacturers set the maximum retail price themselves. The order does restrict how fast they can raise it: a manufacturer cannot increase the maximum retail price of a drug by more than ten percent of the maximum retail price during the preceding twelve months. What the retailer earns on these products depends on the price the distributor charges the shop, which is a matter of trade terms between them.

The order also requires every retailer and dealer to display the price list and any supplementary price list supplied by the manufacturer in a conspicuous part of the premises, so that anyone who wants to consult it can. The rules were written with the customer in mind, and they leave very little room for a chemist to play with prices on essential medicines.

### What that does to a shop's margin, in a worked example

Here is where most people misread the 16 percent, and where the owner's comment started to make sense to me.

Picture a scheduled medicine whose average price to retailer is ₹100. The ceiling price is ₹116, before local taxes. If the shop buys at ₹100 and sells at ₹116, it earns ₹16. As a markup on cost, that is 16 percent. As a margin on the selling price, it is ₹16 out of ₹116, which is about 13.8 percent. The two figures describe the same rupees, and a retailer who confuses them will overestimate his profit every time.

Now say the shop down the road offers ten percent off on medicines, and our shop matches it to keep customers. Ten percent off ₹116 is ₹11.60, so the medicine now sells for ₹104.40. The shop still paid ₹100. Its earning has fallen from ₹16 to ₹4.40, which is about 4.2 percent of the selling price. A discount that sounded modest has taken away almost three quarters of the shop's earning on that strip of tablets.

The owner had worked this out long before I sat on his stool. He told me that he gave discounts only to a handful of long-standing customers with chronic conditions who bought every month, and that he had stopped matching the discount shop on everything. He said he lost a few customers that way and kept most of them, because they trusted his advice more than they valued the small saving.

### Where the room to earn sits

If the regulated part of the shelf earns a capped and fairly thin margin, where does a pharmacy make its living? From what he described, and from what I've since seen in other chemist shops, the answer sits in a few places.

**The non-scheduled range.** Medicines outside the price-control list, along with over-the-counter products, can carry better trade terms, though they vary a great deal by brand and distributor.

**Generic medicines.** Many chemists stock generics alongside branded medicines, and the trade terms on them can differ from branded products. The government's own generic medicine programme, the Pradhan Mantri Bhartiya Janaushadhi Pariyojana, lets individual entrepreneurs and partnerships open a Janaushadhi Kendra. According to the [Janaushadhi FAQ](https://janaushadhi.gov.in/faq), an applicant needs their own or properly leased space, proof of securing a registered pharmacist, documents showing the financial capacity to run the store, and a drug licence in the name of the Kendra.

**Allied products.** Baby care, personal care, health supplements, diabetic supplies, bandages, thermometers and small medical devices fill a lot of shelf space in many pharmacies. They are not price-controlled the same way scheduled medicines are, and they bring in customers who are not ill.

**Volume and repeat business.** This was the point he kept returning to. A customer who buys blood pressure tablets every month for years is worth far more than the margin on a single strip suggests. In a small town those customers are loyal, and loyalty is the real asset of the shop.

### The parts of the day that cost money

Watching the counter that afternoon, I started noticing the costs that never appear in a distributor's margin sheet.

**The credit book.** The owner kept a small notebook of customers who paid at the end of the month, or whenever their wages or crop money came in. In a small town, refusing credit to a regular family can lose you that family for good. Giving it freely can leave you short of cash to pay your own distributor. He managed it by knowing each name in the book personally and setting a quiet limit for each household in his head. When a balance grew past that limit, he would mention it gently the next time the customer came in, before giving more.

**The refrigerator.** Some medicines need cold storage, and the fridge in the corner ran day and night. Power cuts in a small town are a real risk to that stock, so the shop had a backup arrangement for the fridge. Electricity and backup power for that fridge are a fixed cost that a stationery shop of the same size would never carry.

**The hours.** A pharmacy in a small town is often the only place open late when a child spikes a fever. The owner told me he had been woken more than once by someone knocking on the shutter at night. There was no extra charge for it, because in his mind those knocks were part of why the town trusted him, and he was right, but those hours cost him sleep and staff time.

**Returns and near-expiry stock.** He spent part of each week sorting medicines close to their expiry date, deciding which could go back to the distributor under their terms and which would simply be a loss. Every shop has its own arrangement with its suppliers on returns, and he had negotiated his carefully over the years.

None of these costs is dramatic on its own. Together they explain why a capped margin on paper turns into a much thinner one in the cash drawer, and why small town retail business economics depend so heavily on the owner's daily discipline.

### How he thought about his shelf

I asked him how he thought about the different parts of his shelf, and his answer, pieced together over that afternoon, maps onto a simple comparison. The table below uses qualitative ranges on purpose. The only exact figure comes from the official order. For every other category, trade terms vary so widely by brand, distributor, town and volume that any single number would mislead you.

| Retail category | Typical margin range | What drives it |
|---|---|---|
| Scheduled medicines (price-controlled) | Capped: the ceiling price builds in a 16 percent margin on the average price to retailer | DPCO 2013 ceiling price formula; discounts given to customers come straight out of it |
| Non-scheduled branded medicines | Moderate, varies by brand | Manufacturer sets the MRP; the shop's margin depends on distributor trade terms |
| Generic medicines | Moderate to wider, varies by supplier | Purchase price from the supplier, and how many customers accept a generic |
| Over-the-counter and allied health products | Moderate to wider | Brand terms, competition from general stores and online sellers |
| Medical devices and diabetic supplies | Varies widely | Brand, technology, and whether customers compare prices online |
| Kirana staples, for comparison | Thin | Intense price comparison and very high volume |

The owner's summary of this table was better than mine. "On the essential medicines, the government sets my margin. On everything else, my distributor and my customers set it together. My job is to make sure the shop survives on both."

## The lesson that applies far beyond pharmacies

The owner said one more thing that afternoon that I have repeated to many shopkeepers since: "Margin percentage is what the distributor talks about. Money in my hand at the end of the month is what my family eats."

### The first of the small town pharmacy business lessons: margin percentage is half the story

A percentage tells you how much you earn each time an item sells. It says nothing about how often it sells. And in a shop, the second number matters as much as the first.

Picture two shelves, each holding ₹10,000 worth of stock at cost. On the first shelf, the products earn ₹30 for every ₹100 of cost, but they sell slowly, and the whole shelf turns over only four times in a year. That shelf earns 10,000 × 0.30 × 4, which is ₹12,000 over the year. On the second shelf, the products earn only ₹12 for every ₹100 of cost, but they sell quickly, turning over twenty times a year. That shelf earns 10,000 × 0.12 × 20, which is ₹24,000 over the year.

The shelf with the lower margin earned twice as much. It also carried far less risk, because stock that sells every couple of weeks doesn't sit long enough to expire, gather dust or go out of fashion.

Of all the retail margin lessons from small business owners I've collected, this one matters most, and the pharmacy made it vivid because medicines expire. A slow-moving product with a fat margin is a trap if it reaches its expiry date on your shelf. A fast-moving product with a thin margin keeps the cash cycling.

### Small town pharmacy business lessons on stock and expiry

The owner kept an expiry register and checked it every week. He told me that the biggest leaks in a pharmacy were expired stock that couldn't be returned, slow items bought in bulk to get a better rate, and credit given to customers who took months to pay.

He had a simple discipline about bulk buying. A distributor would often offer a better rate on a larger quantity. He took it only for medicines he was sure would sell well before their expiry, the monthly tablets his regular customers depended on. For everything else he bought in smaller quantities, even at a slightly worse rate, because a few rupees saved per strip meant nothing if a box expired on the shelf.

That is a lesson for every shopkeeper, whatever the product. Small town retail business economics reward buying what moves and avoiding what sits. The discount for buying in bulk only counts if the bulk sells in time.

### Trust is the margin you can't see on a bill

The other lesson is about what customers pay for. People came to his counter for medicines, and they also came for advice. The older man with the pain balm question, the farmer's son asking about a cheaper version, the mother with the fever syrup: each of them valued the owner's judgement. The young woman with the pregnancy test valued something else as well: he served her quickly, said nothing beyond what she asked, and put the box in a plain paper bag without being told. In a town where everyone knows everyone, that kind of discretion is remembered for years, and it is the reason some customers walk past a nearer shop to reach his. That trust brought them back, and it let him decline the discount war without losing most of his customers.

In a small town healthcare retail business, trust is the product. A chemist who gives honest advice, stocks what people need, opens when people need it and treats a nervous customer with discretion becomes part of the town's health system. The margin on any single transaction may be thin. The value of the relationship over years is large.

### How the lesson travels to other shops

Take the same thinking to a kirana. Rice, oil, sugar and atta carry thin margins and printed or widely known prices, much like the scheduled medicines. A kirana owner can't move those prices far without losing customers. What he controls is the rest: snacks, local brands, home delivery, the reliability of his stock, and the credit he extends. A kirana that understands this stops fighting over the price of rice and earns its living on everything around it.

Take it to a hardware shop. Branded cement and pipes are compared on price constantly, so the margin on them is thin. The shop earns more on fittings, tools, small accessories and advice: the customer who asks which tap will last, buys the one recommended, and comes back for everything else. A mobile accessories shop works the same way. Phones carry slim margins because every customer can check the price online in seconds. Covers, chargers, screen guards and repair services carry wider ones.

In each case, the pattern is the pharmacy's pattern. Some products bring the customer in at a price the shop doesn't control. Other products and services, sold to the same customer on the same visit, pay the bills. Once a shopkeeper sees his shelf this way, the small business pricing lessons follow naturally: protect the products that pay the bills, keep the price-sensitive ones competitive, and never let a discount war spread from the first group into the second.

The traffic builders and the earners are often different products, and a shop needs both.

## Where most retail owners get margin wrong

Over the months since that afternoon, I've collected retail margin lessons from small business owners who got this wrong, and the same few mistakes repeat in shops of every kind. Each one is easier to see once you have the pharmacy example in mind.

**Confusing markup with margin.** This is the commonest error I meet. A shopkeeper buys at ₹100, sells at ₹125, and thinks he is earning 25 percent. He is earning 25 percent on cost, which is a markup. On the selling price, he is earning ₹25 out of ₹125, which is 20 percent. When he then gives a ten percent discount, thinking he still has plenty of room, he is in for a surprise. Ten percent off ₹125 is ₹12.50, which leaves him ₹12.50 of his original ₹25. Half his earning is gone.

Every one of the small business pricing lessons I teach starts here. Decide whether you are talking about markup or margin, and do your discount sums on the selling price.

**Matching every discount.** Discounts spread through a market quickly. One shop starts, the next shop matches, and soon everyone is selling at a lower price and earning less, with no one gaining customers. The pharmacy owner's approach, discounts for a defined group of loyal customers and none for the rest, is a sound local retail business margin strategy for most small shops. Compete on reliability, advice, availability and service. Use price as a targeted tool, never a blanket one.

**Ignoring how long stock sits.** As the two-shelf example showed, a high margin on a slow-moving product can earn less than a low margin on a fast one. Many shopkeepers judge products by the margin the distributor quotes, without asking how long the stock will sit. Ask both questions every time.

**Forgetting the hidden costs of each sale.** Every rupee of margin has to cover rent, power, staff, the shopkeeper's own time, the cost of credit given to customers and the cost of stock that expires, breaks or goes unsold. A shop that earns a healthy margin on paper can still struggle if credit customers pay late and expired stock piles up in the back room.

**Letting credit run without a limit.** In a town, credit is part of how retail works, and refusing it entirely is rarely realistic. But credit is money the shop has already spent on stock and not yet received. A shopkeeper who sells ₹50,000 of goods on credit in a month has lent ₹50,000 to his customers, interest free, while he may be paying his own supplier on fixed dates. The pharmacy owner's quiet limit for each household is a habit every retailer can copy. Know who owes you, how much, and for how long, and treat any balance that keeps growing as a warning rather than a favour.

**Never pricing what you do control.** Many shopkeepers put a fixed markup on everything, often the same percentage, because it is easy. The products where the shop has real pricing freedom, such as its own packed goods, services, delivery or a specialised item no one else in town stocks, deserve a deliberate price based on what customers value and what competitors charge. Pricing these with care is where a small shop can recover the margin it loses on the price-controlled or heavily compared items.

**Treating every customer the same.** The pharmacy owner treated a regular monthly customer differently from a one-time buyer. That is the right instinct. A local retail business margin strategy that rewards loyalty, through a small discount, home delivery or simply reserving stock, protects the most valuable customers without cutting margin for everyone.

## What I changed in my own advice after this

I've advised retailers across many kinds of shops for years, and before that afternoon I would have told most of them to chase better margins from their suppliers. I still think that matters. But the pharmacy owner taught me to change the order of my questions, and I want to share the changes because they are practical.

**I ask about turnover before I ask about margin.** When a shopkeeper shows me his best-margin products, I now ask how often each one sells. Very often the products he is proudest of turn out to be the slow ones, and the real earners are the boring items that leave the shelf every day.

**I ask what the owner doesn't control.** Every business has prices it can't set. For a chemist, it is the ceiling price on essential medicines. For a petrol pump owner, it is the fuel price. For a kirana, it is the printed MRP on packaged goods and the price the shop next door charges for rice. I now ask every owner to list what they don't control, and then to put their energy into what they do: stock, service, credit, timings and trust.

**I talk about registration and credit earlier.** Many retailers I meet have never registered their business formally because they assume the schemes are for manufacturers. For retail, that assumption is outdated. The Reserve Bank's [FAQ on MSME lending](https://www.rbi.org.in/Scripts/FAQView.aspx?Id=84) explains that the Ministry of MSME, through an office memorandum dated July 2, 2021, decided to include retail and wholesale trade as MSMEs for the limited purpose of priority sector lending, and allowed them to register on the Udyam Registration Portal.

So Udyam registration for a pharmacy business is possible and useful. A chemist shop is a retail trader, and it can register on the [Udyam registration portal](https://udyamregistration.gov.in/UdyamRegistration.aspx) using the Aadhaar number of the proprietor, or of the managing partner for a partnership firm. The registration does not give a retailer every benefit a manufacturer gets. What it does give, under the 2021 memorandum, is recognition for priority sector lending, which can matter when a pharmacy owner wants a loan for stock or for a second outlet. I now suggest Udyam registration for a pharmacy business early, as part of setting up properly, along with the drug licence and, once the turnover requires it, registration on the [GST portal](https://www.gst.gov.in/).

**I ask for a one-page margin sheet every month.** The most practical change is a simple exercise I now give every retailer. Once a month, list your main product groups on one page. Next to each, write three things: what you sold in the month, what that stock cost you, and roughly how many days the stock sits before it sells.

Picture a small shop that does this for three groups. The first group sold ₹60,000 worth of goods that cost ₹54,000, so it earned ₹6,000, about 10 percent of sales, and stock sits about a week. The second group sold ₹30,000 worth that cost ₹22,500, earning ₹7,500, which is 25 percent of sales, with stock sitting about two months. The third group sold ₹10,000 worth that cost ₹6,000, earning ₹4,000, or 40 percent, but stock sits for more than four months. Altogether the shop sold ₹1,00,000 and earned ₹17,500 before rent and other costs.

That single page shows the owner where his money comes from and where it gets stuck. The first group is the traffic builder, thin margin and fast stock. The second earns the most in rupees. The third looks wonderful on a margin sheet, but the money in it is tied up for months, and the owner should ask whether he is buying too much of it. This is the page I wish every shopkeeper kept, and it takes less than an hour a month once the habit is in place.

**I'm clearer about the licence before anyone signs a lease.** A pharmacy can't open on enthusiasm. Retail sale of medicines needs a drug licence from the state drug control department under the Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940 and the Rules of 1945. Delhi's Department of Drugs Control sets out its [procedure for obtaining licences](https://drugs.delhi.gov.in/drugs/procedures-obtaining-licences): a licence on Form 20 is issued for retail sale of allopathic drugs other than those in Schedules C, C(1) and X, and a licence on Form 21 covers retail sale of drugs in Schedules C and C(1). The same guidelines require premises of not less than 10 square metres for these licences, and an applicant for retail sale needs a registered pharmacist, with proof of qualification. Other states follow the same Act and Rules with their own procedures and portals, so check with your state's drug control department before you sign a lease or buy stock.

**I'm more honest about who should open a pharmacy.** Pharmacy business ideas in India attract plenty of people because the demand looks endless. The demand is steady, but the business is regulated, the margins on essential medicines are capped by the ceiling price, and the work requires a qualified pharmacist and real responsibility. I now tell founders plainly: if you are not a pharmacist, you will need one on the premises, and you must understand that a mistake at the counter can harm someone. If that responsibility excites you, a pharmacy can become a respected and durable business. If it doesn't, choose something else.

There is a money side to this honesty too. A pharmacy has to carry a wide range of stock from the first day, because a customer who comes in with a prescription and finds two of the four medicines missing may not come back. Carrying such a range ties up a meaningful amount of cash before the shop earns its first rupee, and much of it is stock with an expiry date. A new chemist also has to build relationships with distributors, who will usually extend better terms to a shop with a track record. In the early months, the owner pays more attention to cash than to margin, because the margin on regulated medicines is already capped and the cash is what keeps the shelves full. Plan the working capital with the same seriousness as the licence, and talk to your bank about it before you open, with your registration documents in hand.

**I rank steady-demand retail by how much trust it needs.** The pharmacy also changed how I advise people choosing a retail business. Retail business ideas with steady demand, such as medicines, groceries, hardware, stationery near schools, and agricultural inputs in farming areas, all survive slow economic periods better than fashion or luxury retail, because people need these things regardless. What separates the winners among them is trust: honest advice, reliable stock and fair dealing.

When a founder asks me for retail business ideas with steady demand, I now rank them by how much trust they require and reward. A pharmacy sits at the top. Hardware and agricultural inputs come close, because customers rely on advice. Groceries need trust in a different way: consistent quality and fair weighing.

**I look harder at the shops around the pharmacy.** Beyond the pharmacy itself, the conversation made me look again at the wider small town healthcare retail business space. Diagnostic sample collection points, medical equipment rental for patients recovering at home, diabetic and elder-care supply stores, and optical shops all sit close to the pharmacy model. Each of them depends on trust, steady repeat demand and a clear understanding of the regulations that apply. Each of them also needs its own licences and qualified staff, so research the specific rules before committing.

## A closing word on learning from the people actually doing it

I have sat through seminars, read books and worked with consultants who talk about retail margins in clean, confident terms. Very few of them taught me as much as one afternoon on a stool beside a chemist's counter in a small town. Lessons from a real small business owner come with the scars attached, and that is why they stick.

The best lessons usually come this way: sideways, in passing, from someone who has been doing the work long enough to stop thinking about it as theory. The pharmacy owner was only explaining his day, with no plan to teach anyone, and in doing so he gave me a sharper understanding of margin than I had before, and I've passed it on to many retailers since.

If you run a shop of any kind, sit with these for a week. Pick your ten best-selling items and your ten highest-margin items, and see how many appear on both lists. The answer may change how you buy next month.
