# The MSME loan mistake for first time founders that sinks a new business

The token number on the screen in the visa office had been stuck for
forty minutes when the man beside me started talking. He ran a small
fabrication unit, he said, and he was applying to visit his son. Then,
because waiting rooms make people honest, he told me the part he was
still angry about. He had taken a loan for his unit, a good one, from
a proper bank, under a proper scheme. The loan had nearly closed his
business inside a year.

He had a plan, a sanction letter and a clean record, and he had done
everything the forms asked of him. What he had also done was borrow
the full amount the bank offered, start paying instalments in the
third month, and spend the money on machines while his customers paid
him sixty days late. By the time the orders arrived, the cash had gone
into instalments and the machines were sitting idle on some days and
running on others.

I've heard some version of that story from founders more times than
I'd like. I sit with people starting MSMEs, people registering under
Startup India, and people bootstrapping something small and stubborn,
and the MSME loan mistake for first time founders keeps showing up in
the same shape. I went home from that waiting room and wrote this
down, because the pattern is avoidable and almost nobody explains it
before the loan is signed.

## The MSME loan mistake for first time founders, named plainly

Here is the mistake in one sentence: the founder sizes the loan to the
sanction letter and the business plan, when it should be sized to the
cash the business has already proven it can produce.

Everything else follows from that. The bank looks at your projections,
your collateral or guarantee cover, your Udyam certificate and your
credit history, and it tells you how much it is willing to lend. That
figure answers the bank's question, which is whether you are a
reasonable risk. It says nothing about your question, which is how
much debt your business can carry while it is still finding its first
steady customers.

First time founder loan mistakes almost always grow from treating
those two figures as the same number. The sanction feels like approval
of the plan. The founder thinks, "If the bank trusts me with this
much, I must be able to handle it." So the full amount gets drawn, the
instalments get fixed, and the business now has a monthly obligation
sized for the version of itself that exists in the spreadsheet.

There is a second half to the mistake, and it often travels with the
first. Founders use a term loan, which is meant for long-life assets
like machinery or fit-out, to cover running costs like stock, salaries
and rent. The term loan has a fixed repayment schedule. Running costs
need money that can flex up and down with the business. When you pay
for flexible needs with rigid money, you get squeezed from both sides:
the instalment never moves, and the running costs keep coming back
every month.

Put those two halves together and you have the most common shape of
small business debt mistakes I see. A loan that is too big for proven
cash flow, used for the wrong kind of expense, with repayments that
start before the revenue does. Any one of those can be survived. All
three at once is what closes units.

A quick self-test helps here. Answer these three questions honestly
before you sign anything. Could the business pay this instalment next
month, from the money it earns today, without touching savings? Is any
part of this loan going to pay for stock, salaries or rent? Does the
first instalment fall due before the business expects its first steady
customers? If the first answer is no, or either of the other two is
yes, you're standing at the edge of the mistake. You can still borrow,
once the amount, the type or the timing has had another look before
the paperwork goes in.

I'd add that the self-test is uncomfortable on purpose. Most founders
I meet want to hear that their plan is fine, and the instinct is to
soften the answers until they pass. Resist that. The questions take
five minutes, and the loan will be with you for years.

## Why it happens to smart, careful people

I want to be fair to the founders who make this mistake, because they
are rarely reckless. In my experience, they are often the most careful
people in the room. They prepared the project report. They registered
properly. They kept their personal credit clean for years so that this
day would go smoothly. The mistake comes from a few specific
pressures, and knowing them is half the protection.

### The sanction letter feels like a verdict

A sanction letter carries weight. It has a bank's name on it, a
reference number and a figure that is usually higher than the founder
expected. It is natural to read it as a judgement on the business. The
figure mostly reflects how comfortable the lender feels about its own
risk, and lenders protect themselves through collateral, guarantee
schemes and the personal liability of the borrower. A bank can be
comfortable lending you an amount that your business will struggle to
repay, because the bank has other ways to recover it. You don't.

### Projections are written on the founder's best day

Every project report I've ever reviewed was written by someone
hopeful. That's fine, hope is why people start businesses. The trouble
is that the loan amount often gets anchored to the projected revenue
in month six or month twelve, and the repayment schedule assumes those
projections arrive on time. When the first customers take longer to
find you, or pay slower than you expected, the instalments don't wait
for them.

### "Take it now, it may not be offered later"

I hear this one constantly. A relative, a consultant or a well-meaning
branch officer says that loans are hard to get, so take the maximum
while it is on the table. There is a grain of truth in it. Credit can
tighten. But borrowing extra money today to protect against a
hypothetical future shortage means paying interest every month on cash
you don't need, and that interest is a certainty while the shortage is
only a possibility.

### The interest feels small next to the dream

Founders look at the rate, compare it with what friends paid, and
decide it is reasonable. They rarely calculate the instalment as a
share of their monthly gross margin, which is the only comparison that
matters. A rate can be perfectly fair and the instalment can still be
too heavy for a business in its first year.

### The gap between buying and earning goes unplanned

Most new businesses spend first and earn later. You buy stock, then
sell it. You do the work, then raise the invoice, then wait for
payment. That gap is where working capital loan mistakes MSME founders
make start to hurt, because the gap needs its own funding. When the
term loan is already spent on assets and the gap appears, the founder
starts using personal savings, credit cards or informal borrowing to
plug it, and the debt starts stacking in layers.

None of these pressures needs a foolish founder. They need an
ordinary, optimistic person who hasn't seen the pattern before. That's
exactly who a first-time founder is.

### The family money that never makes it onto the page

There's one more pressure I see in almost every first-time founder's
file, and it rarely appears in the project report. Before the bank
loan, there was money from family. A brother put in some savings, a
parent sold a little gold, an uncle lent an amount "whenever you can
return it." That money feels softer than a bank loan, so founders
leave it off the page when they work out what the business can carry.

In practice it behaves like any other debt, often with less warning.
Family money comes with expectations, timelines that shift without
warning, and the very real pressure of a wedding or a medical bill on
the other side of the family. When a founder sizes a bank loan as if
the family money doesn't exist, the business ends up carrying two sets
of obligations and only one of them is written down.

So put it on the page. Write the amount, write who it belongs to, and
write the most honest answer you can to when they'll want it back. If
the answer is "probably within two years," treat it as a loan with a
repayment schedule, because sooner or later it will behave like one.
Small business debt mistakes often start here, with a debt the founder
didn't think of as debt.

### Mistakes taking a business loan that I'd avoid on day one

Beyond the main mistake, there's a short list of smaller mistakes
taking a business loan that I see often enough to name. None of them
will sink you on its own. Together they make the main mistake more
likely.

- Signing without reading the repayment schedule line by line,
  including when the first instalment falls due.
- Not asking about processing fees, guarantee fees, insurance add-ons
  and prepayment charges, all of which change the true cost.
- Mixing personal and business accounts, which makes it impossible to
  see what the business can really pay.
- Giving personal guarantees for amounts you haven't sized against
  proven cash flow.
- Choosing a lender because the branch is near home, without comparing
  what other lenders offer for the same scheme.
- Letting a consultant inflate the project report to get a bigger
  sanction. A larger sanction isn't a win if you can't repay it.

Each of these is easy to avoid in the week before you sign and hard to
undo in the year after. That's the whole lesson of first time founder
loan mistakes: they're cheap to prevent and expensive to correct.

## What a healthy loan-to-revenue ratio actually looks like

I'll say this up front: there is no single official ratio that tells
you how much business loan is too much. Banks use their own assessment
methods, which differ between lenders and change with the type of
facility. What I can give you is the way I work it out with founders
across the table, which has served them well and which you can do on
paper in an evening.

### Start from gross margin, not revenue

Revenue is the money customers pay you. Gross margin is what's left
after you pay for the goods or direct costs that went into what you
sold. Your instalment has to come out of margin, along with rent,
salaries, electricity and your own living costs. So the first number
you need is your monthly gross margin, and if you're not sure how to
calculate it, that is the first thing to fix before you borrow
anything.

### Use proven margin, not projected margin

If the business is already running, take the average monthly gross
margin from your last few months of real sales, and use your weakest
recent month as a sanity check. If the business hasn't started yet,
you have no proven margin, and that tells you something important:
borrow for the specific assets you can't start without, keep the
amount as small as the plan allows, and build the rest from early
sales.

### My own rule of thumb for the instalment

This is my personal working rule, and I want to be clear that it's a
habit from my own consulting work and not a banking norm or a
statistic. I like to see the total monthly instalment on all business
loans sit comfortably inside what the business can pay from proven
margin after fixed costs, with enough left over to survive a bad
month. When a founder's instalment would eat most of what's left after
rent and salaries, I tell them the loan is too large, whatever the
sanction letter says.

### A worked illustration

Picture a small food-packaging unit. These figures are an illustration
to show the arithmetic, not data from a real business.

| Line in the monthly picture | Amount (illustrative) |
|---|---|
| Average monthly sales over the last few months | ₹4,00,000 |
| Direct cost of goods sold | ₹2,60,000 |
| Gross margin | ₹1,40,000 |
| Rent, salaries, power and other fixed costs | ₹95,000 |
| Left before any loan instalment | ₹45,000 |
| Founder's own minimum living draw | ₹25,000 |
| Room for instalments | ₹20,000 |

In this sketch, the business can carry instalments of about ₹20,000 a
month on a normal month, and less on a weak one. If the bank sanctions
a loan whose instalment is ₹38,000, the founder would be paying almost
double what the business can afford, and the gap would come from
savings, from delayed supplier payments or from new borrowing. The
sanction might be entirely correct from the bank's side. It would
still be the wrong loan for this business at this stage.

Business loan repayment planning starts exactly here: one honest page
that shows what the business has proven it can pay, set against what
the loan will demand. If you can't fill that page in, you're not ready
to sign.

### Seasonal and lumpy businesses need a harder test

The average-month method above works well for a business with steady
sales. Many Indian small businesses don't have steady sales. A sweet
shop does a large share of its year around festivals. A school-uniform
supplier has a few heavy months and a long quiet stretch. A contractor
gets paid in large, irregular cheques with long gaps between them.

If your business looks like that, the average lies to you. The
instalment falls due every month, including the quiet ones, so the
test has to be run on your quiet months, not your average. Work out
what the business can pay in its weakest stretch, and size the loan to
that. Then ask the bank whether the repayment can be structured around
your cycle, with larger repayments after the busy season. Some lenders
will consider this for working capital, and asking costs you nothing.

The other habit that helps seasonal founders is setting aside a fixed
share of every busy-month rupee into a separate account that only pays
instalments. It feels painful in the good months. It's what lets you
sleep in the slow ones.

### Match the loan type to the purpose

A term loan suits an asset that will keep earning for years, like a
machine or a delivery vehicle. A working capital facility such as a
cash credit or overdraft suits the swings in stock and receivables,
because you draw and repay as the business moves. When founders use
the wrong type, the healthy-looking ratio on paper breaks in practice.
Ask the bank for both, sized separately, and tell them plainly what
each one is for.

### How much business loan is too much, in plain terms

If I had to give one answer to how much business loan is too much, it
would be this: any amount whose instalments you can only pay if the
plan goes right. A healthy loan is one you can still pay in a slow
month, from money the business has already shown it can make.
Everything above that line is a bet on your projections, and you
should place that bet knowingly, in a small amount, if at all.

There's one more angle worth saying out loud. Debt can be a perfectly
good tool. Many good businesses in this country were built with
sensible MSME loans, and a well-sized loan at the right moment can be
the difference between staying tiny and growing. The schemes above
exist because policymakers know that small enterprises need credit.
The aim is to borrow at a size and in a form that the business can
carry, and then to borrow more later, once you've shown you can repay.

## The MSME loan schemes in India worth understanding first

Before you walk into a branch, know what exists. MSME loan schemes in
India are better than most first-time founders assume, and several
were designed specifically for people without collateral or a long
track record. Each has rules that change from time to time, so read
the current terms on the official page before relying on any figure
here.

### Look at your own credit record before the bank does

Every lender you approach will pull your credit report, and for a
first-time founder that usually means your personal report, since the
business has no history yet. The Reserve Bank's rules require each
credit information company to give you a free full credit report once
a year, so there's no reason to walk in blind. Get yours, read it line
by line, and fix any errors before you apply. An old credit card
dispute or a loan you closed but the bank never marked as closed can
slow down an application that would otherwise go through cleanly.

### Udyam registration and loan eligibility

Start with registration. Udyam is the government's free, Aadhaar-based
registration for micro, small and medium enterprises, and you do it
yourself on the [official Udyam registration
portal](https://udyamregistration.gov.in/). Many banks ask for the
Udyam certificate when they process an MSME loan, and it's the
document that places you inside the MSME framework that priority
lending and guarantee schemes rely on. The link between Udyam
registration and loan eligibility is practical more than legal:
lenders and schemes use it to confirm you are the kind of enterprise
they are allowed to support. It costs nothing, so do it before you
apply.

### Pradhan Mantri Mudra Yojana

Mudra loans are for non-farm micro enterprises and are given by banks,
small finance banks, NBFCs and microfinance institutions. At the time
of writing, the categories on the [Mudra scheme's official
site](https://www.mudra.org.in/) are Shishu for loans up to ₹50,000,
Kishor for above ₹50,000 up to ₹5 lakh, Tarun for above ₹5 lakh up to
₹10 lakh, and Tarun Plus for above ₹10 lakh up to ₹20 lakh for
borrowers who have taken and repaid an earlier Tarun loan. The tiers
exist for a reason. Starting in a smaller tier and moving up after a
clean repayment record is how the scheme expects you to grow, and it's
a good discipline anyway.

### The credit guarantee scheme for micro and small enterprises

The Credit Guarantee Fund Trust for Micro and Small Enterprises, which
you'll see written as CGTMSE, works by giving the lending bank a
guarantee rather than lending to you directly, so that the bank can
lend to micro and small enterprises without asking for collateral or a
third-party guarantee. The [CGTMSE website](https://www.cgtmse.in/)
lists the current cover limits and fees. For a first-time founder
without property to pledge, this is often the scheme that makes a bank
loan possible at all, and the guarantee fee is part of the real cost
of the loan, so ask about it upfront.

### SIDBI loan schemes for startups and small units

The Small Industries Development Bank of India works across MSME
finance, partly through lending directly and partly through supporting
the banks and institutions that lend to small businesses. Its
programmes change over time, so the right move is to read the current
offerings on the [SIDBI site](https://www.sidbi.in/en/) rather than
trusting a summary. SIDBI loan schemes for startups are worth a look
if your business is past the very first stage and has some track
record, because the terms are built around MSME realities.

### Stand-Up India

[Stand-Up India](https://www.standupmitra.in/) supports bank loans
between ₹10 lakh and ₹1 crore for greenfield enterprises set up by
women entrepreneurs and by entrepreneurs from Scheduled Castes and
Scheduled Tribes. If you fit, it's worth knowing, though the amounts
are larger than many first-time founders should borrow in year one,
which makes the sizing question even more important.

### PMEGP

The Prime Minister's Employment Generation Programme, run through the
Khadi and Village Industries Commission and other agencies, links a
bank loan with a government margin-money subsidy for new micro
enterprises. The subsidy makes it attractive, and it comes with
application steps, training requirements and processing time that
founders often underestimate. Plan for the timeline before you plan
around the money.

### If you're a registered startup as well as an MSME

Some founders reading this will have recognition from the Department
for Promotion of Industry and Internal Trade through the [Startup
India portal](https://www.startupindia.gov.in/), on top of their Udyam
registration. The two are different things. Udyam places you in the
MSME framework that most bank lending schemes refer to. Startup India
recognition is for newer entities working on innovation or a scalable
model, and it opens a separate set of benefits, including access to
certain government-backed funding routes that work through investors
and institutions rather than through your local branch.

For a first-time founder, the practical lesson is that recognition
doesn't change the sizing rule. A recognised startup with a heavy
instalment and no proven margin is in the same trouble as any other
small business. If anything, startup founders are more exposed to the
main mistake, because the culture around startups celebrates raising
money, and a large loan can feel like the same kind of achievement.
Keep the two ideas apart: raising equity shares risk with investors,
while a loan keeps every rupee of the risk on you.

### A side-by-side view

| Loan scheme | Typical use case | Who it fits best |
|---|---|---|
| Mudra (Shishu to Tarun Plus) | Small working capital or equipment for a non-farm micro enterprise | First-time founders starting small and building a repayment record |
| CGTMSE-backed bank loan | Term loan or working capital without collateral | Micro and small enterprises with a viable plan and no property to pledge |
| SIDBI programmes | MSME finance on terms designed for small businesses | Units with some operating history looking to expand |
| Stand-Up India | Greenfield enterprise funding in the ₹10 lakh to ₹1 crore range | Women founders and SC/ST founders starting a new venture |
| PMEGP | New micro enterprise with a subsidy component | Founders willing to go through the application and training process |
| Bank cash credit or overdraft | Day-to-day swings in stock and receivables | Running businesses with regular sales and a clear working capital cycle |

The table is a starting map. Your branch officer will know which of
these the bank actively processes, and that matters, because a scheme
that a branch rarely handles can take much longer in practice.

### Questions to ask the branch before you sign

Whichever scheme you choose, the conversation at the branch decides
how the loan will feel for the next few years. These are the questions
I make every founder I work with ask, and write the answers down.

- When does the first instalment fall due, and is there a moratorium
  period before repayment starts?
- What is the total cost, including processing fee, guarantee fee,
  documentation charges and any insurance the bank adds?
- Is the rate fixed or linked to a benchmark that can move, and how
  often can it change?
- What are the charges if I repay early, fully or in part?
- If the business needs working capital as well, can it be sanctioned
  as a separate facility instead of being folded into the term loan?
- What happens, specifically, if I need to restructure the repayment
  in a bad quarter?

That fifth question is where most working capital loan mistakes MSME
founders make get prevented. When the bank understands that you need
two kinds of money for two kinds of purpose, it can structure the
facility properly, and you avoid the trap of paying for monthly stock
out of a loan meant for a machine.

A good branch officer will answer all six without hesitation. If the
answers are vague, ask again, or ask another lender. The point is to
understand the loan in full before your signature makes it permanent.

## How to recover if this MSME loan mistake for first time founders already happened

If you're reading this with a loan you already regret, I want to start
with something plain: this is fixable far more often than it feels at
two in the morning. The founders who recover tend to do the same few
things, and they do them early.

### Write the honest page first

Before you call anyone, sit down and write the page from the section
above. Proven monthly gross margin, fixed costs, your minimum draw,
and the instalment. Seeing the gap as a number takes some of the fear
out of it, and it's the page you'll need for every conversation that
follows.

### Talk to the bank before you miss a payment

This is the single most useful step, and the one founders delay the
longest. Banks have options for restructuring, changing the repayment
schedule or adjusting a facility, and those conversations go far
better while your record is still clean. The day after a missed
instalment, you're negotiating from a weaker position. Go to the
branch, show the page, and ask what can change. The Reserve Bank of
India sets the framework banks work within, and its [official
website](https://www.rbi.org.in/) publishes the circulars and the
customer grievance routes if you feel a request has been handled
unfairly.

### Separate the term loan from the working capital gap

If you used term-loan money for running costs, the fix is usually to
get a proper working capital facility sized to your actual cycle, so
that the term loan stops carrying expenses it was never designed for.
This is a real conversation with the bank, and it's easier to have
when you can show your sales, purchase and collection patterns for the
last few months.

### Stop the debt from stacking

The dangerous moment comes when founders cover a business loan
instalment with a credit card, a personal loan or informal borrowing
from relatives at uncertain terms. Each layer seems temporary, and
each one adds its own instalment. If you're already doing this, list
every obligation in one place, with the amount, the rate and the due
date. Small business debt mistakes compound quietly, and putting them
all on one page is how you stop the compounding.

### Collect faster before you cut deeper

Founders in trouble usually start by cutting costs, which helps, but
the faster lever is often collections. Shorter credit terms for new
customers, polite and regular follow-up on overdue invoices, a small
discount for early payment where margins allow it. Every week you
shave off the collection cycle is money you don't need to borrow.

### Sell or pause the idle asset if you must

If the loan bought a machine that's running at a fraction of capacity,
consider renting out spare capacity to another unit, taking job work,
or in the hardest cases selling the asset to reduce the loan. It's a
painful call. It's also sometimes the call that saves the rest of the
business.

### What a recovery tends to look like, month by month

Picture a founder who has realised, about eight months in, that the
instalment is too heavy. This is a sketch of how a recovery usually
unfolds when it goes well, not a record of one specific business.

In the first month, the founder writes the honest page and lists every
obligation, including the family money and the credit card that's been
quietly covering gaps. The total is frightening, and seeing it is the
turning point. In the second month, they go to the branch with the
page, explain the mismatch, and ask about restructuring and a separate
working capital line. The bank asks for statements and a revised plan,
and the founder provides them within the week.

Over the next few months, the founder tightens collections, stops
using the credit card for business expenses, and pays down the most
expensive informal debt first. Sales don't jump, but the cash cycle
shortens and the monthly squeeze eases. By the end of the first year
after the realisation, the instalment fits inside what the business
earns, the founder has a working capital facility sized to the real
cycle, and the family money has a written repayment plan everyone
agreed to.

None of that requires luck. It requires the founder to look at the
numbers early and to have one uncomfortable conversation at the bank
before the record turns bad. Business loan repayment planning done
late still works, as long as it gets done.

### Use the formal grievance routes for payment delays

If your own customers are other businesses and they're paying you
late, look at the Samadhaan delayed payment portal run under the MSME
ministry, which exists for micro and small enterprises chasing overdue
payments from buyers. It takes time, and it gives your follow-up some
weight and it's one of the few tools designed specifically for this
problem.

I also want to say something to the founders who'll read this and feel
embarrassed because they've already made the mistake. Please don't.
The man in the visa office was a capable, hardworking person who'd
built a real business. He made a sizing error, recovered from it with
an honest conversation at his bank and two hard years of discipline,
and his unit was still running when he told me the story. The mistake
is common. Recovery is common too, for the founders who face the
numbers early.

## A closing word on borrowing responsibly

When the token number finally moved and the man got up, he said one
more thing that stayed with me. "I should have borrowed half and asked
for the rest later." That's the whole lesson of the MSME loan mistake
for first time founders, compressed into one sentence by someone who
paid for it.

Borrow for what you can't start without. Size the instalment to what
the business has already proven it can earn. Use term loans for
long-life assets and working capital facilities for the cycle.
Register on Udyam before you apply, understand the schemes before you
walk in, and talk to your bank early if anything starts to slip.
Business loan repayment planning is a page of honest arithmetic, and
it's the most valuable page a first-time founder will ever write.

If you've been through this yourself, either the mistake or the
recovery, I'd really like to hear how it went. Write to me or leave a
comment with your version of the story: the scheme you used, the
moment you realised the instalment was too heavy, the conversation
that turned it around. Somebody reading this next month is about to
sign their first sanction letter, and your story might be the one that
makes them borrow half and ask for the rest later.
