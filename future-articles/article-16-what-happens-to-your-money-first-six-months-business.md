# What actually happens to your money in the first six months of a small business

On an overnight bus a few weeks ago, the film playing on the screen above the aisle froze, and an advertisement filled the gap: a young founder cutting a ribbon, a queue of customers, a bank balance climbing on a phone screen. Thirty seconds, start to success. The passengers around me went back to sleep. I stayed awake thinking about everything that advertisement had left out, because I've sat with enough first-time founders to know where the real story happens. It happens in the months between the ribbon and the queue.

Money in the first six months of a small business behaves in a predictable way. Founders who know the pattern in advance plan for it and get through. Founders who don't tend to discover it in month three, usually at the moment they can least afford the surprise. I want to lay the pattern out plainly so you can see it coming.

## Why the first six months break the budget nobody wrote

Most first-time founders never write a proper budget, and the reason is practical: a real budget needs answers a new founder can't have yet: how long the first customers will take to find you, what a licence will cost once every fee and form is counted, how a supplier's payment terms work in practice. So the founder carries a rough figure in their head and starts.

The trouble is that the rough figure is built from the costs they can picture. The costs that hurt are the ones nobody pictured: the second trip to fix the first installation, the deposit nobody mentioned, the slow fortnight that was supposed to be busy. Startup cost reality for small businesses almost always sits above the founder's first estimate, and in my experience the gap comes from these unlisted items far more often than from any single extravagant purchase.

There is a second, deeper problem, and it explains most of the stress founders feel in this period. Income and expenses move on different clocks. Expenses arrive on fixed dates: rent on the first, salaries on the seventh, the loan instalment on the fifteenth. Income arrives when customers decide to buy, which in a new business is irregular and slower than hoped. Cash flow for a new small business is shaped by that mismatch. A founder can have a promising business and still run short of cash, simply because the bills keep their schedule and the customers don't.

## Where money in the first six months of a small business goes, in order

Following the money month by month makes the pattern easier to see than any single total.

**Month one: setup.** Registration and licence fees, the first stock or equipment, a deposit and the first rent if there is a premises, signage, and a surprising number of small fixes as the setup meets reality. Revenue in this month is usually small enough to ignore when planning.

**Months two and three: the gap.** Setup is mostly paid for, and ongoing costs are now running: rent, utilities, supplies, any staff, marketing to find customers. Revenue is growing, but slowly, because word of mouth takes time to spread. This is where the distance between money going out and money coming in is usually widest. It is the stretch that new business cash flow planning has to be built around, and it is the stretch most rough budgets ignore.

**Months four to six: the turn.** In a business that is working, revenue climbs to meet monthly costs somewhere in this window. The gap narrows month by month, and the founder starts to understand the real rhythm of the business: which days are busy, which products move, which customers return.

That sequence answers the question of where small business money actually goes in the first half-year. Most of it goes into the setup in month one and into covering the gap in months two and three. Very little of it, in a typical new business, comes back before month four.

| Expense category | Typical timing | Common underestimate |
|---|---|---|
| Registration and licences | Month one | Fees and documents found partway through |
| Stock or equipment | Month one, then again early | A second purchase to correct the first |
| Rent and utilities | Every month, fixed dates | How little room there is in a slow month |
| Marketing to find customers | Months one to three | How long it takes to show results |
| Working capital reserve | Should exist from day one | Underfunded, or skipped entirely |

### A sketch with numbers

Picture a founder starting a small home-based food business with, say, ₹2,00,000 saved. She sets aside ₹40,000 for setup: kitchen equipment, FSSAI registration, packaging and her first ingredients. The remaining ₹1,60,000 she keeps as a reserve.

Month one uses the setup budget and another ₹35,000 from the reserve, covering ingredients for small early orders and the small mistakes any new kitchen makes. The reserve stands at ₹1,25,000.

Months two and three draw ₹60,000 more between them. Orders grow steadily, but they still fall short of covering the full monthly cost. At the end of month three the reserve is at its lowest: ₹65,000.

In month four, revenue finally covers the month's costs. Months five and six add a little back, and by the end of the sixth month the reserve has recovered to around ₹75,000.

Now run the same story without the reserve. With only the setup budget, she would have run out of money in month one, long before the business had a chance to work. The reserve is what carries her through the gap to month four. That is its whole purpose.

These are illustrative figures. Your own will differ with your business, your city and your prices. The shape, a deep dip in months two and three followed by a slow recovery, is the part that tends to repeat.

### How the pattern changes with the type of business

The dip appears in almost every new business, but its depth and timing depend on what you're building, and the startup cost reality for small businesses varies a great deal by model.

A service business built on the founder's own skill, such as tutoring, design, consulting or repairs, has the lightest version. Setup costs are small, often little more than tools and a phone, so the six-month story is mostly about how quickly paying clients arrive. Cash flow for a new small business of this kind depends on outreach and referrals far more than on spending, and the reserve mostly covers the founder's own living costs while the client list builds.

A retail business carrying stock has the opposite problem. A large share of its money goes into inventory before a single sale happens, and that money only comes back as the stock sells. If the first order was too large, or the wrong mix, cash stays trapped on the shelves for months. Retailers need a larger reserve than service founders, and they need to buy cautiously until they learn what moves.

A food or small production business sits between the two. Equipment and licensing cost more than a service business needs, revenue depends on reputation spreading through a neighbourhood, and raw materials have to be bought ahead of orders. The dip tends to be moderate but long, because reputation builds slowly.

A business that sells to other businesses faces its own version. It may win a large order early, then wait weeks or months to be paid. Planning a realistic startup cash flow India-based founders can rely on means treating a signed order as revenue only once the money is in the account.

### Two founders, the same idea, two different six months

Picture two founders opening similar small retail shops in the same city in the same month, each with roughly the same savings.

The first splits her money deliberately: enough for setup and a cautious first stock order, with a protected reserve for the slow months. When sales grow more slowly than she hoped in months two and three, the reserve absorbs the shortfall. By month five her sales cover her costs, and by month six she knows which products to reorder and which to drop.

The second spends more on day one: a bigger first order, a nicer fit-out, on the reasoning that a fuller, smarter shop will bring customers in faster. Sales arrive at roughly the same pace as the first shop's. With no reserve, he spends month three looking for an emergency loan to cover rent, and the only lender willing to move quickly charges a punishing rate.

The idea, the market and the starting money were the same. What differed was the order in which the money was spent, and that single choice decided which founder spent month six planning and which spent it scrambling. When people ask me where small business money actually goes wrong, this is usually the answer: too much of it was committed on day one.

## The expense every new founder underestimates

If I could change one habit in every first-time founder, it would be this: fund the working capital reserve properly before spending on anything that can wait.

The reserve is the cash that covers the gap between spending and earning in the early months. It pays the rent when sales are slow, buys the second batch of stock when the first sells faster than expected, and lets the founder say no to a desperate, expensive loan. In the founders I've worked with, an underfunded reserve has ended more promising businesses than any other single cause I can point to.

It gets underfunded because it's invisible. Setup costs produce something you can see: equipment, a painted shopfront, shelves of stock. A reserve sits in a bank account looking like money that could be put to work. So founders spend a little of it on a better sign, a larger first order, a nicer counter, and arrive at month two with far less cushion than they planned.

### How to size the reserve

Small business budgeting for beginners can stay simple and still work. I suggest three steps.

First, list your fixed monthly costs: rent, utilities, salaries, loan instalments, subscriptions, anything that arrives whether or not you sell. Add a modest amount for supplies you'll need regardless of volume.

Second, estimate honestly when revenue will cover those costs. Then assume it will take longer. In the businesses I've sat with, the revenue ramp has almost always run slower than the founder's first guess, so build your estimate around the slower case.

Third, size the reserve to cover the fixed costs for every month until that later break-even point, with a margin for surprises. If your fixed costs are high and your break-even point is far away, the reserve will be large, and that is useful information. It may mean the business needs to start smaller, or that the savings need to grow before you begin.

Keep the reserve in a separate account if you can, and treat it as untouchable for anything except covering a real shortfall. A reserve spent on improvements has stopped being a reserve.

### Where to keep the reserve

The reserve needs to be safe and available within a day or two, because its job is to cover a shortfall when it happens. That rules out putting it anywhere it can lose value or get locked away.

A separate savings or current account works for most founders. Keep it apart from both your personal account and the business's daily operating account, so that dipping into it is a deliberate decision rather than an accident at the counter. Some founders split it in two: a portion in an account they can reach instantly, and the rest in a short deposit that can be broken without much penalty if needed.

Keep it out of shares, long lock-in deposits and any investment promising a quick return. The reserve's job is to be there in month three, at full value, and growth is a secondary concern. A founder who invests the reserve hoping to grow it and then finds it has fallen in value just as the slow months arrive has turned a safety net into a second risk.

### A first-month budget, line by line

For anyone who has never drawn up a budget, here is the list I ask founders to fill in before they spend anything. Your first six months business expenses will mostly fall into these lines.

One-time costs:
- Registration, licences and any professional help with paperwork
- Equipment and tools
- Initial stock or raw materials
- Premises deposit, fit-out and signage
- Initial marketing: printed material, a launch offer, photographs

Monthly costs:
- Rent and utilities
- Salaries or payments to helpers
- Stock or materials to replace what sells
- Phone, internet and software
- Transport and delivery
- Loan instalments, if any
- Marketing to keep finding customers

The reserve:
- Fixed monthly costs multiplied by the number of months until a cautious break-even point, plus a margin for surprises

Fill in every line, even with a rough figure. A line left blank usually means a cost you'll meet unprepared. Small business budgeting for beginners works best when it is honest and complete rather than precise, because a rough figure for every line beats a precise figure for half of them.

## What a healthy first six months actually looks like

Success at this stage looks quieter than most people expect, and it helps to know what to measure so you can recognise it.

A healthy first six months shows a trajectory, and profit can come later. Each month, the gap between what comes in and what goes out is a little narrower than the month before. Repeat customers are appearing alongside one-off sales. And the founder can explain why the numbers look the way they do, which products carry the business, which days are strong, where money leaks.

Most small business financial survival first year advice focuses on growth targets and marketing tactics. In my experience the businesses that survive their first year are distinguished mainly by how they manage cash in the first six months: realistic reserves, honest tracking, and early responses to warning signs. A good product helps enormously, and it still needs enough cash behind it to reach the customers who will love it.

### Tracking money in the first six months of a small business, week by week

The pattern only helps if you can see where your own business sits within it. Four numbers, checked weekly, are enough.

**The reserve balance.** Write it down every week on the same day, regardless of mood. A falling number noticed early is a problem you can manage. A falling number noticed late is a crisis.

**Revenue against your realistic estimate.** Compare actual sales with the cautious estimate you used to size the reserve, rather than the hopeful one you started with. This gives a far more useful reading of whether things are on track.

**The monthly gap.** Revenue minus fixed costs, for the month so far. This single figure shows how fast the reserve is being used or rebuilt.

**Surprises.** Any expense that came in higher than planned, noted individually with the reason. Over six months, this list becomes the most accurate budget you'll ever have for your next venture or your second year.

A founder who tracks these four numbers consistently, even imperfectly, finishes the first six months knowing their business better than most advice could teach them.

### Three checkpoints: months one, three and six

Alongside the weekly numbers, I ask founders to stop at three points and answer a few questions honestly, on paper.

At the end of month one: Did setup cost more than planned, and by how much? Which costs were missing from the budget entirely? Is the reserve still at the level you intended, or did setup eat into it? If it did, refill it or shrink your plans now, before the gap begins.

At the end of month three: Is the monthly gap narrowing, even slightly? Which products or services are bringing in most of the money? Which customers have come back? What would you stop doing if you had to cut one cost tomorrow? This is the most important checkpoint, because it falls at the bottom of the dip, when decisions still have time to work.

At the end of month six: Is revenue covering monthly costs? If not, how far away is that point, and does the reserve last that long? What have you learned about pricing, about your customers and about your own working pattern? What will you do differently in the next six months?

Writing the answers down matters. Memory softens bad months and inflates good ones, and a page written at the time is an honest record you can come back to.

## Warning signs worth taking seriously early

A few signals, noticed early, give you room to correct course before a real crisis forms.

**The reserve falls faster than planned for two months running.** One bad month happens to almost everyone. Two in a row is a pattern. Cut a cost or revisit an assumption now, while there is still cash to work with.

**Revenue and profit get confused.** A month with strong sales can still lose money once every cost is counted, including costs that arrive later. Small business money mistakes early on cluster around this confusion. Track profit from the first month, even roughly, so a strong sales week doesn't hide a weak margin.

**Borrowing to cover routine costs.** Borrowing for a planned piece of equipment is a business decision. Borrowing to pay this month's rent because sales fell short is a warning sign, especially from an informal lender at a high rate. It adds a fixed cost to a business that is already struggling to meet its existing ones.

**Avoiding the numbers.** When the reserve starts to fall, looking at it feels unpleasant, and many founders stop looking. This is the most human mistake on the list and the most expensive, because it switches off the early warning exactly when it matters most.

### Mistakes that make the warning signs worse

Several habits turn a manageable dip into a serious one.

Mixing personal and business money is the most common. When both run through one account, the founder can no longer see the business's real position. Open a separate account in month one, before revenue arrives.

Treating one big order as proof of the model is another. A single large sale is a data point. Scaling stock or staff on the strength of it, before a pattern confirms it, leaves the business exposed when the next month is ordinary.

Underpricing to win the first customers creates a problem that grows over time. Customers who joined at an unsustainably low price resist increases, so the business stays stuck with thin margins long after it no longer needs to buy their loyalty.

Letting small recurring costs pile up unnoticed rounds out the list. A few modest subscriptions and fees, each trivial alone, become a steady drain that rarely gets reviewed in the busy first months. Review every recurring cost at the end of month three and cancel what isn't earning its place.

### Getting paid faster

Much of the cash pressure in these months comes from money that is owed but hasn't arrived. Speeding up collection can ease the gap as much as cutting costs.

Take advance payments where your trade allows it. Custom orders, catering, tailoring and event services can reasonably ask for a deposit before work begins, which funds the materials and filters out customers who aren't serious.

Collect at the moment of delivery. A UPI QR code at the counter or a payment link sent with the order makes paying immediate and easy, and customers who pay on the spot don't need chasing.

Be careful with credit. Extending credit to regular customers or business buyers can win sales, but every rupee on credit is a rupee missing from your account while your own bills keep arriving. Set clear terms, write them down, and follow up on the day payment falls due, politely and without exception.

These habits matter a great deal for new business cash flow planning, because they shorten the gap between doing the work and having the money for it.

### What to do if the reserve runs low anyway

Sometimes the reserve shrinks faster than planned despite good habits. If that happens, act in this order.

First, speak to the people you owe before you miss a payment. A landlord, a supplier or a bank told early about a temporary shortfall is often willing to adjust a date or a term. One told after the payment is missed has far fewer options and far less goodwill.

Second, cut costs that don't produce revenue. Look for subscriptions, unused services and non-essential spending first. Be slow to cut the marketing and stock that bring in sales, since those are what will close the gap.

Third, collect what you're owed. Chase outstanding payments courteously and firmly, and offer a small discount for prompt payment if it brings money in sooner.

Fourth, if you need to borrow, borrow formally. A working capital loan from a bank, on terms designed for small enterprises, costs far less than informal credit, and it comes with a repayment structure you can plan around. Many small business money mistakes early on begin with a quick informal loan taken in a panic.

Finally, revisit your plan. A reserve running low is information. It may mean prices are too low, costs are too high, or the business needs to start smaller than planned. Adjusting early is far cheaper than adjusting late.

### When the problem is the model itself

Most businesses that struggle in these months are slow, and slowness can be survived with a reserve and patience. A smaller number have a deeper problem: the business, as designed, loses money on each sale or can't attract enough customers at a price that covers its costs. Telling the two apart early saves a great deal of money and heartache.

A few signs point to a model problem. Customers who try the product or service rarely return, even when they were satisfied in the moment. Every sale, once its full cost is counted, makes less than it costs to deliver. Raising prices to a sustainable level drives away most customers. Revenue has stopped growing for two or three months while costs stay the same.

If you see these signs, more reserve will only delay the outcome. The useful response is to change something fundamental: the price, the product, the target customer, or the way the business is run. Talk to customers who left and ask why. Look at which part of the business, if any, is working, and consider building around that part alone. Sometimes the honest conclusion is to close the business while the remaining savings can still fund the next attempt. That conclusion is painful, and reaching it at month six with money left is far better than reaching it at month eighteen with nothing.

### What a family safety net changes

There's one factor that shapes these months for many founders and rarely appears in business advice: whether family money stands behind the business as an unofficial reserve.

A founder who knows a parent or sibling could step in during a real emergency feels the dip in months two and three differently from a founder with nothing behind them. There's nothing wrong with having that support, and nothing to be embarrassed about. It does change how much risk you can sensibly take, and it's worth being honest with yourself about whether it exists and how far it stretches.

For a founder without that backstop, the official reserve is the whole margin for error. The budgeting, tracking and early warning habits described above stop being good practice and become essential. If that's your situation, size the reserve more cautiously than you think you need to, start smaller, and treat the first sign of trouble seriously. Your small business financial survival first year plan should be built for the case where no one can rescue you, because in your case that is the real case.

## Where registration and formal credit fit

Your first six months business expenses include some paperwork that pays for itself, and doing it early makes the cash picture easier.

[Udyam registration](https://udyamregistration.gov.in/) is free and done online. It gives the business a formal identity as a micro, small or medium enterprise, and that identity is the starting point for MSME lending schemes, including collateral-free credit backed by government credit guarantees. If your business does need working capital to get through the gap, a registered enterprise can approach a bank, or institutions such as [SIDBI](https://www.sidbi.in/en/) and their partner lenders, on far better terms than an informal lender would offer.

[GST registration](https://www.gst.gov.in/) becomes mandatory once turnover crosses the threshold that applies to your business and state, and some founders register earlier because their customers need GST invoices. Either way, proper invoicing from the start avoids a painful reconstruction later and creates a record of sales that lenders find persuasive.

Two other programmes are worth a look for specific businesses. Small manufacturers can explore [NSIC](https://www.nsic.co.in/), whose raw material assistance can ease the cash tied up in buying inputs. Founders building something new in an incorporated company or LLP may qualify for [Startup India recognition](https://www.startupindia.gov.in/), which carries its own tax and funding provisions for eligible startups. Neither applies to every small business, so check the criteria before planning around them.

### Borrow for the gap, or save longer first?

Founders who find their reserve falls short of what the budget says they need face a choice: borrow the difference, or delay the start until savings catch up. Both can be right, and the deciding question is how predictable the business is.

If the business has a clear, tested model, for instance a skill you have already sold to paying customers, or a product with orders lined up, a modest formal loan sized to cover the gap can make sense. The repayments are a fixed cost, so include them in your monthly figures and size the loan to what the cautious revenue estimate can carry, with room to spare.

If the business is untested, waiting is usually wiser. Borrowing to fund the gap in an unproven idea adds a fixed repayment to the most uncertain months of the venture. A few extra months of saving, or starting smaller as a side project until the idea proves itself, reduces the risk considerably. It's slower, and it means that if the idea needs to change, you can change it without a loan hanging over you.

Whichever you choose, write the decision and the reasoning down. Six months later, it's the most useful page you'll have for judging whether the call was right.

A realistic startup cash flow India-wide also has to account for local rhythms that a generic template misses: festival seasons that bring a surge of sales and a surge of stock purchases before them, monsoon months that slow footfall in some trades, and larger buyers who pay on credit terms that stretch for weeks. Building these into your estimate from the start makes the reserve more accurate and the surprises fewer.

## A closing word on surviving the first stretch

Every business that comes out of its first six months intact has passed through some version of the gap described here. The ones that make it planned for the gap honestly, held a real reserve against it, and watched their numbers closely enough to act early. The tools are within anyone's reach: a realistic estimate, a protected reserve and the habit of looking at the figures each week, especially in the weeks when you'd rather not.

If you are about to start, build the reserve before the shopfront. If you are already in month two or three and the numbers frighten you, look at them anyway, write down what you see, and make one decision this week based on it. Founders who keep looking at their numbers through the hard months tend to find a way through them, and the ones who look away usually find out too late.

That bus advertisement promised a story about opening day, and the business itself is decided in the six quiet months that come after it.
